# MIRIAM

The first email comparing the red rain to Revelation arrived before breakfast.

By ten in the morning there were forty-three.

Most came from strangers. A few came from pastors I knew well enough to disappoint personally. Two were from television producers who wanted me to explain whether the first seal had been opened.

I declined both invitations.

Revelation is a difficult book to teach because everyone arrives thinking they know what it is about.

They usually know a sequence assembled from sermons, novels, charts, films and fragments of the text itself: Antichrist, rapture, mark of the beast, Armageddon, end of the world. Some of those ideas belong to Revelation. Some belong elsewhere in scripture. Some belong to interpretive traditions developed centuries later. Some have been stapled together so often that distinguishing them feels impolite.

My students were kinder than television producers because at least they expected to be corrected.

At eleven I stood in front of nineteen graduate students and wrote one sentence on the board.

**SIMILARITY IS NOT FULFILLMENT.**

A hand went up in the back.

“Does that mean you think the blood rain is irrelevant?”

“First,” I said, “stop calling it blood rain unless you are trying to make a point about language.”

A few people smiled.

“Second, Revelation contains blood imagery. Exodus contains blood imagery. The prophets contain blood imagery. Ancient warfare, sacrifice and plague contain blood imagery. Human beings are not short of reasons to be frightened by red water.”

Another student said, “But Revelation literally has hail and fire mixed with blood.”

“Yes.”

“And now we have red rain at the biggest AI company in America.”

“Meridian is not the biggest AI company in America.”

“Fine. One of them.”

“Still not an argument.”

She leaned back, frustrated.

I knew the feeling. People often mistake skepticism for lack of imagination. The opposite is usually true. If you can imagine enough possible explanations, the first satisfying one has less power over you.

I pulled up the passage on the classroom display.

“We need to distinguish at least three questions,” I said. “What happened physically? What does the event mean socially? And what, if anything, does the resemblance to a sacred text mean theologically?”

A student near the front said, “You just made it sound more relevant.”

“I am not trying to make it irrelevant.”

That quieted them.

I had spent most of my career arguing with two kinds of readers: people who treated Revelation as an encrypted newspaper and people who treated it as an embarrassing appendix Christianity would be better off without.

Both approaches avoided the same difficulty.

The book makes claims about power.

Empire describes itself as permanent. Revelation says it is not.

Markets describe themselves as ordinary. Revelation shows them morally charged.

Violence describes itself as necessary. Revelation makes violence visible from the side of its victims.

The frightening thing about apocalyptic literature is not that monsters may appear. It is that the world you call normal may be the monster already.

My phone buzzed on the lectern.

I ignored it.

“Professor Hale,” said a student named Rebecca, “if events really did start matching Revelation, what evidence would convince you?”

That was a better question than the others.

I considered giving the academic answer: textual sequence, specificity, independence of interpretation, causal improbability.

Instead I said, “I don’t know.”

Rebecca blinked.

“You don’t?”

“No. And anyone who tells you they have a clean test for divine action is selling something.”

The room laughed, but I had not meant it as a joke.

After class I checked my phone.

Three missed calls from Mara Chen.

One message.

**I need the least apocalyptic Revelation scholar in America. Congratulations.**

I called her back.

“You have selected the wrong person,” I said when she answered.

“That’s why I selected you.”

I liked her immediately, which made me suspicious.

“What are you writing?”

“Not writing yet. I’m trying to understand why searches for Revelation spiked six hundred percent after a weather event.”

“Because people enjoy being terrified in familiar language.”

“Can I quote that?”

“No.”

“Worth asking.”

She told me about the automated advisory, the private sensor attribution and Meridian’s unexplained data path. She was careful about what she knew and what she did not. That was rarer than it should have been.

Then she asked about the white horse.

“Why?”

“Because I’ve seen it in posts about Rook.”

I sat down.

“What are the posts saying?”

“That he’s the first rider. White horse, conqueror, crown, all that.”

“And what do you want me to say?”

“What the text actually says.”

I recited it from memory, then stopped myself before I turned the call into a lecture.

“The first rider is given a crown and goes out conquering,” I said. “Interpretation varies. Some Christians have identified the rider with Christ. Others with conquest or imperial power. Some end-times traditions identify him with Antichrist. Revelation itself does not stop and label him for us.”

“So Rook as Antichrist is possible?”

“That is not what I said.”

“I know. I’m stress-testing the sentence.”

“You’re stress-testing my blood pressure.”

She laughed.

I continued. “You can make Julian Rook resemble almost any symbolic figure if you begin by choosing the resemblance. He is powerful, charismatic and associated with technology. That describes half the villains in modern fiction and several people at Davos.”

“What if the pattern keeps going?”

“Then the pattern keeps going.”

“That’s your answer?”

“It is until the evidence changes.”

There was a pause.

Mara said, “You’re not curious?”

“Of course I’m curious.”

“You sound annoyed.”

“Curiosity and annoyance coexist beautifully.”

She asked whether I would appear on record if she eventually wrote a piece about the religious response. I told her yes, provided the piece was about the religious response and not whether God had endorsed a software company.

Before we hung up she said, “Rook’s doing a national address Saturday.”

“I heard.”

“Does that bother you?”

“A private citizen giving a national address?”

“Yes.”

“It bothers my political theology more than my eschatology.”

“Can I quote that?”

“Yes.”

At two I walked across campus to the chapel because my office had become unusable.

The messages kept arriving.

A pastor in Ohio sent a video explaining that iron oxide in rain fulfilled a prophecy because iron symbolized empire.

A man in Arizona sent me a spreadsheet aligning AI product releases with biblical feast days.

A former student asked whether she should withdraw her savings from the bank.

That message frightened me more than the others.

Interpretation becomes real when people act on it.

I called her.

She answered crying.

Her husband had been laid off by an insurance company after his department adopted an automated claims system. Their rent had increased. Their church had started a food pantry. She had seen the red-rain videos and then a livestream in which a preacher said the seals had begun.

She wanted me to tell her it was nonsense.

I could tell her the preacher was reading badly.

I could tell her red rain had physical explanations.

I could tell her Revelation had been used to misread crises for two thousand years.

What I could not honestly tell her was that the world around her was stable.

So I asked about the rent.

We spent twenty minutes on an emergency assistance program I knew through the divinity school.

When the call ended I sat alone in a pew near the back of the chapel.

Sunlight came through the stained glass and broke itself across the floor.

I thought about the question Rebecca had asked.

What evidence would convince you?

Theologically, it was malformed. God was not a hypothesis competing with mineral aerosol.

Professionally, it was dangerous. Scholars are vulnerable to the fantasy that being difficult to convince is the same as being impossible to fool.

Personally, I had no answer.

At four my younger brother sent me a link to a clip of Julian Rook from an interview the previous year.

Rook was talking about unemployment.

“If we make intelligence cheap and leave dignity expensive,” he said, “that’s not a technological failure. That’s a political choice.”

My brother’s message beneath it said:

**He’s right, though.**

I watched the clip twice.

Rook was good.

That was not a moral judgment. It was a technical observation.

He listened to the interviewer as if listening were an action rather than a pause before speech. When he answered, he did not merely address the question. He seemed to identify the fear underneath it and answer that instead.

I understood immediately why people trusted him.

I also understood why that trust worried me.

Not because he sounded like a beast.

Because he sounded like a shepherd.

I closed the video before the metaphor could settle into anything more convenient.

On Saturday, I decided, I would watch his address from beginning to end.

Not to look for prophecy.

To make sure I was not refusing to see something simply because too many foolish people claimed to see it first.
