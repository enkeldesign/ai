# LEAH

The pressure wave reached Virginia after lunch.

We knew it was coming.

That did not make the windows less strange.

I was in the state laboratory watching live footage from Iceland when the barometer trace on the wall rose sharply, fell, then rose again. A few seconds later the long windows along the west side of the room flexed with a soft collective click.

Nobody screamed.

Three people said “wow” at the same time.

The eruption had begun hours earlier beneath the ice north of Vatnajökull after a night of seismic collapse events large enough to be felt across Iceland. The first satellite images showed a plume punching through weather layers and spreading outward in a white-gray umbrella lit from inside by volcanic lightning.

On the news, the scale was impossible to understand.

In the lab, it arrived as pressure.

That helped.

Physical things become less mythic when an instrument records them.

My phone had been vibrating since the memorial.

Aviation warnings. Coastal advisories. Air-quality forecasts. Freight delays. Questions from county offices about whether volcanic material could affect reservoirs. Messages from people who had seen the red-rain story in March and decided I was now the state expert on every unusual thing that fell from the sky.

I was not.

We had an atmospheric chemist named Dr. Salazar for that.

He stood beside me staring at the satellite loop.

“If the sulfur injection is as high as they think, this is going to be around awhile,” he said.

“Ash?”

“Most ash falls out faster. Sulfur to sulfate is the long game.”

“How long?”

“Depends on altitude, mass, circulation. Months. Maybe longer for the aerosol signal.”

He enlarged an image.

The plume top was difficult to estimate because the satellite sensors were saturating in different ways.

“This is not a normal bad day,” he said.

That was as dramatic as Salazar got.

By evening, most transatlantic flights had been rerouted or canceled.

Ash-dispersion models disagreed about where the hazardous concentrations would move. Airlines hated uncertainty more than closure because closure at least created a rule.

Shipping insurance prices jumped.

Cloud providers began shifting workloads away from North Atlantic regions even though the data centers themselves were not threatened. Staff could not move. Replacement parts might not move. Power-market connections were changing. The physical world had reminded the internet it lived somewhere.

At 7:00, the sky over Richmond looked ordinary.

At 7:18, the sunset began turning color.

Not blood red.

That phrase had been ruined for me.

The horizon developed a narrow band of saturated orange under a high pale haze. Above it, purple appeared where there should have been blue-gray. People came out of the lab to photograph it even though Salazar kept explaining that the most dramatic global aerosol effects would take longer to develop.

“This part is mostly ordinary scattering plus whatever high cloud we already had,” he said.

Nobody put their phone away.

The world does not wait for the most scientifically pure version of an omen.

Overnight, Icelandic authorities reported repeated caldera-collapse earthquakes.

Live feeds showed ash turning midday into dusk near the eruption. Lightning crawled inside the column. Floodwater from melting ice tore through channels toward the coast. Roads disappeared. Farms were evacuated.

The geological explanation was complete enough to be reassuring.

Magma withdrawal. Collapse. Explosive interaction with ice and water. Ash. Gas. Pressure waves.

Nothing violated physics.

At 4:30 the next morning I was sent north with a mobile sampling team.

Not because Virginia expected centimeters of ash. Because precipitation was forecast to intercept the leading edge of the aerosol plume, and every governor on the East Coast had suddenly remembered atmospheric deposition existed.

By noon we were in Pennsylvania under a sky that looked overexposed.

Sunlight had a flat quality, not dark but thinned. The sun itself showed through high haze as a hard white disk you could glance toward longer than instinct normally allowed.

“Don’t,” Salazar said when he caught me doing it.

“I know.”

“No, you don’t. You’re an environmental engineer. You people think PPE is a personality flaw.”

We set collectors on the roof of a county building.

The first rain arrived after three.

Clear.

I was relieved enough to laugh.

Salazar heard me.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You thought it would be red.”

“I did not.”

“You absolutely did.”

The samples showed sulfate increasing over background, but nothing dangerous at ground level in that location. Fine mineral material was present, mostly ordinary regional aerosol with a small volcanic signature.

No plague.

No poison.

A large volcano had erupted on a planet with an atmosphere.

That was enough.

At 5:12 Mara called.

“Tell me why the moon is going to turn red.”

“It isn’t necessarily.”

“Thank you, Miriam.”

“I assume that was an insult.”

“She’s been saying the same thing for twelve hours.”

“Aerosols can change optical effects. Sunsets can get much redder. The moon can look unusual through haze. But if you write that the volcano is fulfilling a blood-moon prophecy I will deny knowing you.”

“I’m writing about why everyone else is writing that.”

“That is how you people escape responsibility.”

“Yes.”

Her voice changed.

“Leah, did you know the Compact shifted North Atlantic freight three days ago?”

I stopped packing bottles.

“What?”

“Not publicly. A resilience recommendation moved some medical stockpiles and aviation-support capacity away from the corridor.”

“Because of the seismic swarm?”

“That’s what I’m trying to establish.”

“Three days is not impossible.”

“I know.”

“Volcanoes show unrest.”

“I know.”

“Then what bothers you?”

“The recommendation was more specific than the public risk level. And one of its data sources appears to have been an experimental Icelandic geophysical feed that wasn’t part of the Compact agreement.”

I closed my eyes.

Of course.

“Another fallback service?” I asked.

“Maybe.”

“Another undocumented broker?”

“Maybe.”

“You’re calling me so I’ll tell you the geology can still be ordinary.”

“Yes.”

“It can.”

“Does that make you feel better?”

“No.”

“Me neither.”

After the call I looked west.

The rain had stopped. The high haze remained.

My phone showed photographs from Europe, Iceland, ships under ash-dark sky, canceled flights stacked across airport boards. A video from Reykjavík showed people standing in the street during one of the collapse earthquakes, looking upward as if the shaking had come from the sky.

At 8:03 the first major American network ran a graphic comparing the previous six months to the seals in Revelation.

They invited Miriam Hale to respond.

She said the graphic was irresponsible.

The clip went viral.

At 8:40 another network used the same graphic with a question mark.

By midnight, the question mark was doing very little work.

I sat in a hotel room labeling water samples while the television showed the eruption again.

Every time the caldera collapsed, instruments recorded it.

Every time ash rose, satellites saw it.

Every strange color had an optical explanation.

That should have been enough.

Then the anchor said Meridian had begun diverting transatlantic capacity thirty-six hours before Iceland raised its highest eruption warning.

I stopped writing.

On the screen, Julian Rook was being asked how Meridian knew.

He answered, “We didn’t know. We prepared for a low-probability event because the cost of preparing was small.”

That was a perfectly good explanation.

I believed him.

Mostly.
