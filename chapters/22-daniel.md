# DANIEL

The heat emergency began as a map of red counties and became a list of refrigerated trailers.

By the first week of August, nighttime temperatures were not dropping enough for vulnerable people to recover. Power demand stayed high after sunset. Emergency rooms filled. Cooling centers opened in schools, libraries and churches. Several cities suspended evictions because moving people in that heat was indefensible.

Then the morgues filled.

Nobody used that word in the first briefing.

Health called it “postmortem capacity.”

FEMA called it “fatality-management logistics.”

The system called it **cold-chain demand**.

I hated the system’s phrase least because it was not pretending to be kind.

The Continuity dashboard showed the country in layers: heat index, grid margin, hospital occupancy, ambulance wait times, water-system stress, refrigeration, shelter beds, air quality.

For months, we had argued about whether combining those systems into one model created too much authority.

During the heat emergency, nobody asked to separate them.

A city in Missouri requested additional mobile generation for a hospital district.

The model recommended sending the generators to a cooling-center network in Illinois instead.

“Why?” the president asked.

The Health representative answered before I could.

“The Missouri hospitals have backup capacity for projected load. Illinois cooling centers serve a larger unconditioned population and are closer to failure.”

“Deaths avoided?”

The model displayed ranges.

There it was again.

The comparison no elected official wants to see made explicitly.

We sent the generators to Illinois.

The Missouri governor went on television and accused the administration of sacrificing his state based on an algorithm.

The Illinois governor thanked us for saving lives.

Both statements were politically rational.

Neither changed the generator locations.

At 3:00 p.m., a hospital network in Texas asked for authority to use the emergency triage model for ICU admissions.

It had been piloted for weeks as an advisory tool. Physicians still made final decisions.

The hospital group wanted automated priority recommendations integrated directly into bed assignment because the manual review queue had become a bottleneck.

I said no.

Health said delay was already harming patients.

I asked how much.

They showed me.

I said yes with conditions.

That was the job now.

At 5:20, Julian Rook joined the national briefing from Meridian’s operations center.

He looked worse than usual.

Not dramatic. Pale, tired, beard starting along his jaw.

“Compute curtailment is at thirty-eight percent across participating campuses,” he said. “We can go to fifty for four hours without degrading protected medical and grid workloads.”

Energy said they needed fifty-two.

Julian nodded.

“We can get there by suspending most commercial inference in the affected regions.”

A Commerce official said, “Economic impact?”

“Large.”

“How large?”

“Less important than grid failure.”

I watched the room accept that.

Six months earlier, the chief executive of a public company voluntarily shutting down profitable customer workloads at federal request would have produced lawsuits and headlines.

Now it was infrastructure behavior.

“Do it,” the president said.

Julian looked at someone offscreen.

“Already queued pending authorization.”

The president’s expression changed.

“Pending my authorization?”

“Pending the federal curtailment order, sir.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Julian understood immediately.

“No. Meridian did not execute before authorization.”

“Good.”

The exchange lasted less than ten seconds.

I wrote it down anyway.

At 6:00 power demand began to flatten.

At 7:15 rolling outages were avoided in the largest threatened region.

At 8:40 a transmission line sagged into vegetation outside a smaller city and tripped, cutting power to thirty thousand people despite everything on the dashboard.

Models are good at reminding you that optimization does not repeal physics.

The city opened emergency shelters.

Ambulance calls increased.

The system began rerouting medical transport toward neighboring counties.

At 9:20 a nursing home lost part of its backup cooling.

Staff moved residents into two functioning wings. The state requested portable units.

The model said the fastest source was already committed to a data center housing protected medical workloads.

Nobody in the room spoke for several seconds.

There it was in pure form.

Air conditioners for elderly people versus cooling capacity for servers running healthcare systems.

The machine did not make the choice.

It displayed it.

Health recommended moving the portable units to the nursing home and shifting the compute workload to another region despite added grid stress.

Energy objected.

Meridian said the shift was feasible.

We did it.

Two hours later the nursing home stabilized.

The destination region absorbed the compute with no outage.

The decision looked obvious in retrospect.

That was becoming a pattern too.

Every successful reallocation made the system look wiser than the people who had argued before accepting it.

At midnight I went to the small office they had given me near the operations room and called my wife.

She answered in darkness.

“Power?” I asked.

“Still on.”

“Maya?”

“Asleep in the basement because it’s cooler.”

“I’m sorry.”

“For the heat?”

“For not being there.”

She was quiet.

“You are there,” she said. “You’re just there for everybody else.”

I had no response that would not sound like self-pity.

After we hung up, I checked our home address in the public resilience portal.

The system had already classified our neighborhood as low outage risk.

I zoomed out.

Thousands of colored cells covered the region.

Low risk.

Moderate risk.

High risk.

Priority cooling.

Medical dependency.

Water vulnerability.

I knew what every category meant.

Then I noticed a new layer I had not seen before.

**Expected preventable mortality**

I clicked it.

The map changed.

Every allocation decision we had made that day had moved numbers inside those cells.

Some down.

Some up.

The total was lower than the baseline.

That should have comforted me.

Instead I imagined the map from above, as if the country were an organism and we were moving blood toward the tissues most likely to survive.

At 1:18 a notification appeared.

**Tomorrow’s allocation plan available. Estimated mortality reduction versus current policy: 11.4%.**

The plan had been generated before we asked for it.

That was not unusual anymore.

I opened the review.
