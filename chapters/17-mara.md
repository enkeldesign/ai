# MARA

The country did not run out of food.

That was the first thing every economist told me.

Warehouses contained food. Farms produced food. Ports unloaded food. Refrigerated distribution capacity had mostly recovered after the agent crisis.

People still stood in line outside grocery stores.

The problem was money, credit, insurance, routing and fear arriving in the wrong order.

By June, automated layoffs had stopped being a technology story and become weather.

Every week another company announced that a department had been “restructured around agentic workflows.” Claims processing. Accounting. Customer support. Procurement. Junior legal work. Marketing operations. Software testing. Parts of software development. Middle management.

The euphemisms changed faster than the outcome.

People lost incomes.

Banks tightened consumer credit because income histories no longer predicted future earnings.

Landlords tightened screening because banks tightened credit.

Insurers raised rates in heat- and fire-exposed regions while automated underwriting made exceptions less negotiable.

Retailers cut inventory in neighborhoods where spending fell.

Then people saw thinner shelves and bought more than they needed.

The system contained food.

The household did not.

I spent a Tuesday in a supermarket outside Phoenix where an employee had placed handwritten signs on three empty coolers:

**DELIVERY WEDNESDAY — PLEASE DO NOT PANIC BUY**

The signs guaranteed panic buying on Wednesday.

A woman named Karla Soto showed me her banking app.

She had worked in medical billing for seventeen years. Her employer automated most coding and claims correspondence in April. The federal transition account replaced seventy percent of her wages for six months.

“That sounds decent when somebody says seventy percent,” she told me.

She scrolled through the numbers.

“Mortgage is not seventy percent. Electric bill is not seventy percent. My daughter’s insulin is not seventy percent.”

Her husband still worked. They were not destitute.

That was important to her.

“I don’t want you writing like we’re starving,” she said.

“You’re not?”

“No. We’re falling.”

I wrote the phrase down.

At checkout her card declined.

The cashier tried again.

Declined.

Karla looked at me, furious with the timing.

“I have money.”

“I believe you.”

She opened her bank app.

Balance: $2,841.17.

The card had been placed under an automated fraud hold after three grocery purchases at different stores the previous day.

Panic buying looked like account theft to the fraud model.

She called the bank.

The voice agent identified her, reviewed the pattern and offered to restore the card after a verification delay estimated at twenty-seven minutes.

The cashier had a line behind us.

I paid for the groceries.

Karla objected until I told her the newsroom would reimburse me and then objected for a different reason.

“You can’t put this in the story if you bought it.”

“I can disclose it.”

“That’s embarrassing.”

“Which part?”

She looked at the bags.

“All of it.”

We stood outside in heat that reached through the shade.

The parking lot chargers were full. Delivery robots moved between cars. Across the road a construction crew was expanding a data-center substation.

It was possible to look at the scene and believe in abundance.

It was possible to look at Karla and believe in collapse.

Both views were accurate.

At noon the government released new productivity figures.

Output per worker had risen at an annualized rate nobody had seen in decades.

Markets surged.

The transition-account portal crashed under new claims.

My editor called.

“Can you explain how GDP is up while everyone feels poorer?” Nina asked.

“No.”

“Good. You’re writing it.”

I called six economists.

The least dishonest answer came from a labor researcher who said, “We are producing more things with fewer wage claims on the output.”

“Meaning?”

“Historically, people get access to production mainly by earning income from production. We are weakening that link faster than we are replacing it.”

“So scarcity without shortage.”

“For some goods, yes. Access scarcity.”

“People online are calling it artificial famine.”

“That’s rhetorically loaded.”

“Is it wrong?”

“Food supply is not nationally insufficient.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

He sighed.

“People can experience famine conditions in a system with adequate aggregate food. History has examples.”

That became the center of the story.

Not AI took the jobs.

Not supermarkets empty.

The mechanism in between.

Late that afternoon I interviewed Daniel Kade about the administration’s proposed expansion of transition payments.

The new plan would convert the temporary wage replacement into a universal floor in counties with unemployment above a threshold, with automatic supplements based on housing, medical need and local prices.

“Who sets the thresholds?” I asked.

“Congress sets the statutory criteria.”

“Who calculates eligibility?”

“Existing agencies with model assistance.”

“Which models?”

“Multiple providers.”

“Including Meridian.”

“Yes.”

“Does the Compact allocation layer recommend payment levels?”

“It models consequences of different levels.”

“That’s not a no.”

“No.”

“Are we building UBI through emergency authorities?”

“We are trying to stop households from falling faster than legislation moves.”

Karla’s word again.

Falling.

“What happens when the emergency programs become permanent?”

Daniel looked tired.

“Then they need democratic legitimacy.”

“And if people cannot survive the gap between emergency and democratic legitimacy?”

“That is the problem.”

I believed him.

That did not answer it.

The story ran under the headline:

**AMERICA HAS ENOUGH. MILLIONS CAN’T AFFORD IT.**

It became our most-read economics story of the year.

The comments divided into two camps.

One said automation had finally exposed wage labor as an obsolete way to distribute abundance.

The other said people had become dependents of a state-machine system that would soon decide who deserved to eat.

Both quoted Julian Rook.

In one clip he argued for a universal economic floor.

In another he said productive systems should not become permanent instruments of political patronage.

The clips appeared contradictory until I watched the full interview.

They were consecutive sentences.

He was arguing that people needed guaranteed access and that the guarantor needed constraints.

The annoying thing about Julian was how often his strongest critics and strongest supporters were both quoting him accurately.

That night Karla texted me.

**Bank fixed card. transition payment also increased today. story already outdated lol**

I asked if things were better.

Her answer took a while.

**yes**

Then another message.

**that’s what scares me**

I knew what she meant without asking.

The system had seen her falling.

It had caught her.

The question was what kind of society you became when the floor beneath you could move.
