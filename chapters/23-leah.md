# LEAH

People think heat kills dramatically.

Sometimes it does.

More often it looks like a bad decision made by a tired body.

A man waits too long to refill a prescription because the bus is late. A woman turns off the air conditioner because she is afraid of the bill. A worker finishes one more hour outside because the shift bonus matters. A nursing home moves thirty residents into two cooled rooms and misses the one person whose medications make dehydration worse.

By the second week of August, I had stopped thinking of the emergency as weather.

It was a pressure test on every system that kept water inside people and electricity inside buildings.

The state sent me south to a town whose drinking-water intake had begun pulling warmer, lower-quality water from a shrinking reservoir.

The treatment plant could handle it.

Then a thunderstorm hit the watershed after weeks of heat and flushed nutrients and organic material into the source.

The plant had to increase treatment.

Treatment required chemicals.

Chemical deliveries were still unreliable.

The backup generator had been run so often that maintenance was overdue.

The operators were short two people because one had heat exhaustion and one had left for a better-paying job maintaining a data center.

No single failure mattered.

All of them mattered.

I arrived at 6:30 in the morning and the air was already twenty-nine degrees.

The plant manager, a woman named Carla Nguyen, handed me a bottle of water before saying hello.

“Drink it,” she said.

“I have one.”

“Drink mine too.”

That was how people greeted one another that month.

The reservoir smelled wrong near the intake. Algae, warm mud, vegetation decomposing along the exposed banks.

Dissolved oxygen near the surface looked acceptable. Deeper readings were worse.

Fish had collected near a tributary where cooler water still entered.

A few floated dead along the shoreline.

The images would look apocalyptic on television by afternoon.

The mechanism was not.

Warm water holds less oxygen. Nutrient load drives biological growth. Decomposition consumes oxygen. Low water concentrates problems. Heat stresses fish before anything reaches a threshold dramatic enough for a headline.

I took samples while sweat ran under my vest.

Carla’s radio crackled.

“Leah, we’ve got another pressure drop on the east side.”

A water-main break.

The soil had dried and shifted. Old pipe did what old pipe does when conditions move outside its comfortable range.

We drove into town.

The break had opened a brown stream along a residential street. Children were standing behind police tape filming it.

A city crew worked under a shade tent between excavations because the wet-bulb safety rule now required mandatory rest cycles.

One man complained loudly about the rule until another worker vomited beside the truck.

The city had enough pipe.

What it did not have was enough people who could safely install it at noon.

The Continuity system recommended reducing pressure in two adjoining zones to limit water loss until evening.

That would leave several apartment buildings on upper floors with weak service.

One of the buildings housed a dialysis clinic.

The model flagged it automatically.

A tanker was rerouted.

I watched the sequence happen on Carla’s tablet.

Break detected.

Pressure zone identified.

Critical users mapped.

Tanker available.

Route calculated.

Human approvals appeared as green checks beside each action.

The whole chain took ninety seconds.

Ten years earlier, the same response might have taken an hour of phone calls.

I could not honestly want the old system back.

At noon, I went to the dialysis clinic.

The tanker had arrived.

Patients sat in a cooled waiting room while staff verified water quality.

A nurse recognized the state logo on my vest and said, “Tell whoever runs that system thank you.”

I almost said no one person ran it.

That was not what she meant.

“Okay,” I said.

Outside the clinic, a man was sitting on the curb in the shade of an ambulance.

I thought he was waiting for someone until I saw the paramedic kneeling beside him.

Construction worker. Forty-eight. Confused, hot skin, pulse fast.

They loaded him into the ambulance.

He died later at the hospital.

I learned his name from the incident report: Samuel Ortiz.

His death did not appear on the water dashboard because he had not died from water-system failure.

It did not appear on the grid dashboard because his workplace had power.

It did not appear on the labor dashboard because his employer had followed the mandated rest schedule.

It appeared in the mortality layer as one additional heat-related death.

That was the category.

A life becomes administratively manageable once it has a category.

At 3:00 the state asked me to inspect a cooling center after complaints about drinking-water taste.

Nothing was wrong with the water.

The center had switched to stored supplies during the pressure reduction, and the warmer temperature made residual chlorine more noticeable.

People were frightened because everything tasted like evidence now.

A woman in her seventies showed me the alert on her phone.

It said she qualified for priority transportation to a cooling center because of age, address and a registered cardiovascular condition.

“How does it know my heart?” she asked.

“You probably opted into medical emergency sharing.”

“I don’t remember doing that.”

“Most people don’t remember permissions after the third screen.”

She looked at the phone.

“It sent a car.”

“Good.”

“Is it?”

I understood the question.

“Yes,” I said. “Today it is.”

She laughed.

“That’s not comforting.”

“No.”

At sunset I returned to the reservoir.

The temperature had barely dropped.

Along the shoreline, volunteers collected dead fish into plastic bins so they would not decompose in the shallows.

The scene smelled terrible.

A local reporter asked whether the deaths were caused by climate change, the drought, pollution, the data centers upstream or the heat wave.

“Yes,” I said.

He lowered his microphone.

“I need you to pick one.”

“Then you need a different system.”

I explained the chain as simply as I could.

He listened, then asked, “So who’s responsible?”

That was the question everyone had begun asking.

Science is good at causes and often bad at defendants.

I looked across the reservoir.

A flock of birds had gathered near the remaining cool-water inflow. Beyond them, transmission lines disappeared into the haze.

“Responsibility isn’t a contaminant you can sample,” I said.

He did not use that line.

I was glad.

Driving back to the motel, I passed a church sign with black removable letters:

**HOW LONG, O LORD?**

I knew enough Bible to recognize it as a common lament, not proof of anything.

The next sign, two miles later, belonged to a data center.

**NOW HIRING — CRITICAL FACILITIES TECHNICIANS — $42/HR + BONUS**

For a moment the two signs felt connected.

Then I made myself stop doing that.

At the motel, the state mortality dashboard showed one hundred and eighty-seven confirmed heat-related deaths for the day, with the estimate expected to rise.

Somewhere inside the model, tomorrow’s allocation plan was already moving water, power, vehicles and people to bring that number down.

I wanted it to succeed.

I also wanted to know what it would become if success was the only argument we had left.
