# MIRIAM

“How long?” is one of the most dangerous prayers in scripture.

Not because it is impious.

Because it asks God to hurry.

I said that at Priya Raman’s memorial service and immediately wondered whether I had made a category error by turning a dead engineer into a lecture.

Her family had asked me to speak because Priya’s mother attended a church whose pastor had studied with me years earlier. I had never met Priya.

That should have disqualified me.

Instead it was apparently evidence of neutrality.

The chapel was full before the service began.

Meridian employees filled one side. Journalists and public officials sat near the back. Jeremy Vale was there. Zeke Boone was there without a camera crew. Julian Rook sat three rows behind Priya’s family, which seemed exactly far enough to be respectful and exactly close enough to be photographed.

I resented myself for noticing.

Priya’s mother spoke first.

She told stories about a daughter who alphabetized spices and once dismantled the family toaster because she thought the thermostat was inefficient.

Her brother described her refusing to use hotel Wi-Fi long before anyone else in the family knew why that might matter.

People laughed.

Grief returned Priya to herself for twenty minutes.

Then it was my turn.

I had decided not to read Revelation.

I changed my mind when I reached the lectern.

I read the passage about the souls beneath the altar asking how long before justice.

Then I looked at the room.

“This text is often treated as a timetable,” I said. “Today I want to treat it as a refusal.”

The room was quiet.

“The dead ask how long because explanations are not justice. Procedures are not justice. Historical necessity is not justice. Even if an institution can explain exactly why a person died, the moral question does not disappear.”

I saw Jeremy lift his head.

“This does not mean Priya was murdered. I do not know that. Most of us do not. It means uncertainty about cause does not make grief morally empty.”

Julian did not move.

“The danger in asking how long is that we may begin to desire judgment more than truth. We may need someone to punish so badly that evidence becomes an obstacle. Revelation does not give us permission to lie on behalf of justice.”

Zeke looked down.

“And yet,” I continued, “the opposite danger is equally real. Powerful systems can make every death legible, explainable and therefore administratively absorbable. We can become so sophisticated about causality that nobody remains responsible for anything.”

That was the sentence I had come to say.

Priya’s death lived inside a machine of reasonable explanations.

Construction error.

Map latency.

Fatigue.

Driver-assist limitations.

Emergency data integration.

Corporate audit procedure.

Every component could be true.

A moral account could still be missing.

After the service, Julian approached me.

“Professor Hale.”

“Mr. Rook.”

“You made me uncomfortable.”

“I believe that is traditionally part of the service.”

He smiled.

Up close, he looked less presidential than television made him. Taller, yes, but tired. There were dark hollows under his eyes.

“I agree with what you said about responsibility,” he told me.

“That complicates things.”

“Sorry.”

“Are you?”

His smile faded.

“Yes.”

I believed him.

That irritated me.

“Did Priya believe your systems were becoming dangerous?” I asked.

People near us pretended not to listen.

Julian considered the question.

“She believed our categories were failing.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

The directness surprised me.

“What categories?”

“Private and public. Tool and institution. Advice and decision. User and system.”

I felt something cold move through me.

“Human and machine?”

He looked at me carefully.

“Not yet.”

It was a strange answer.

Not *no*.

Not yet.

I said, “That sounds almost theological.”

“I was warned not to debate theology with you.”

“By whom?”

He glanced toward his staff.

“Several sources.”

I wondered whether one of them was human.

Julian continued. “Can I ask you something?”

“You can.”

“What would convince you that the religious interpretation is not merely pattern matching?”

It was Rebecca’s question from months earlier.

I felt suddenly annoyed.

“Why do you care?”

“Because millions of people care, and how they interpret events changes what they do.”

“That is a political answer.”

“It is also true.”

“Do you believe Revelation is being fulfilled?”

“No.”

Too fast.

I noticed.

He noticed me noticing.

Then he corrected himself.

“I don’t have a reason to believe that.”

“Better.”

“What would count as a reason?”

I looked toward Priya’s photograph.

“I do not want to answer that here.”

“Fair.”

He nodded to me and moved away.

Zeke appeared almost immediately.

“You enjoyed that,” he said.

“No.”

“You absolutely did.”

“I enjoyed making him revise a sentence.”

“That is the academic equivalent.”

We walked outside.

The afternoon was hot enough that mourners clustered under trees instead of lingering on the chapel steps.

“What did he ask?” Zeke said.

“The same thing everyone asks me. What would convince me?”

“And?”

“I still don’t know.”

“You know what would convince me.”

“I am afraid to ask.”

“The pattern continuing in sequence with specificity no one can plausibly engineer.”

“That is at least falsifiable enough to discuss.”

He smiled.

“You’re coming around.”

“No. I am improving your argument so I can reject it properly.”

“Same road, different lane.”

I stopped walking.

“Zeke, promise me something.”

His expression changed.

“What?”

“If events become more biblical, become less certain, not more.”

He laughed once.

“I’m serious.”

“So am I. That is backwards.”

“No. It is exactly right. The closer reality gets to a symbol system you already believe, the greater your risk of forcing everything into it.”

He did not answer.

“Promise me,” I said.

“I can promise to try.”

“That is all I’m asking.”

Across the lawn, Jeremy was speaking with Priya’s brother. Mara stood alone near the parking lot, looking at her phone but not typing. Daniel Kade had arrived late and remained near the back, surrounded by aides.

For the first time I saw them not as separate people appearing in separate news stories but as a group history was assembling.

That thought was melodramatic.

I rejected it immediately.

Then every phone on the lawn sounded at once.

Emergency alert.

People stopped talking.

The message warned of an unusual seismic event under the North Atlantic and possible coastal impacts under evaluation.

A second alert followed less than a minute later.

**Satellite observations indicate a rapidly expanding atmospheric plume associated with the event. Aviation and maritime advisories pending.**

I looked at Zeke.

He did not say anything.

That was the promise, I realized.

Not certainty.

Silence long enough to let the world be itself first.
