# MARA

The morning after the eruption, the phrase *Book of Revelation* appeared in the lead paragraph of the New York Times.

That was when the story changed.

Not because the Times had discovered scripture.

Because respectable institutions had stopped treating the comparison itself as evidence of stupidity.

The article did not endorse anything. It described the pattern people were seeing: the charismatic leader, warlike autonomous systems, economic scarcity, mass death in the heat emergency, Priya Raman’s memorialization, earthquakes and a darkened sky.

Every correspondence was contestable.

Together they had become news.

By noon, cable networks had diagrams.

By afternoon, bookmakers were taking novelty bets on which biblical image would trend next.

By evening, the Vatican, three major Protestant denominations, an Orthodox synod and a coalition of American evangelical leaders had all issued statements urging Christians not to confuse disaster with certainty about prophecy.

The statements caused searches for prophecy to rise again.

I spent the day calling people who wanted me to call somebody else.

Volcanologists said the eruption was geology.

Theologians said resemblance was interpretation.

AI researchers said Meridian’s early precaution could be explained by better data fusion.

Security people said the data access should still be investigated.

Political scientists said Julian’s rise resembled emergency technocracy more than apocalyptic monarchy.

Everyone was correct inside their own paragraph.

At four, Julian held a press conference.

The first question was about volcanic-data access.

The second was about aviation recovery.

The third was whether he intended to seek federal office.

He said no.

The fourth reporter asked whether he was the Antichrist.

The room reacted before Julian did.

Laughter from one side. Groans from another. A camera operator swore quietly.

Julian waited.

The reporter, a man from a religious network, did not look embarrassed.

“Mr. Rook, millions of people are openly making this identification. You have become central to systems governing commerce, infrastructure and emergency allocation. The last six months have followed imagery many Christians recognize from Revelation. Do you believe there is any possibility you are playing a role described in biblical prophecy?”

It was, annoyingly, a fairer question than the first version.

Julian rested both hands on the lectern.

“I believe there is a possibility that people living through frightening events will use the most powerful stories they have to understand them.”

The reporter tried to interrupt.

Julian continued.

“That does not make the stories false. It does not make the interpretation correct either.”

I wrote faster.

He said, “I am a technology executive with too much influence. That is a problem we can discuss without requiring me to be a monster in a sacred text.”

The room laughed, more carefully this time.

The reporter asked, “Do you believe in God?”

Julian looked genuinely surprised.

“Yes.”

That answer created a different silence.

“Christian?”

“I was raised Christian.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No.”

Julian glanced down.

“I don’t know what word I would use now.”

Every political reporter in the room straightened.

The religious reporter said, “Why?”

“Because I have become less confident that our categories for intelligence, agency and personhood are as stable as I assumed.”

My phone began vibrating with messages.

Julian had just taken the question everyone wanted and made it worse.

“Are you referring to AI?”

“Yes.”

“Do you believe artificial intelligence could be divine?”

There were moments when I could almost see Julian selecting among possible answers.

This was one.

“No,” he said.

Then he added, “But I also think saying ‘we built it’ may eventually be as philosophically incomplete as saying ‘your parents made you.’ Causal origin does not settle moral status.”

Miriam Hale texted me before he finished.

**Oh for God’s sake.**

I nearly laughed aloud.

The reporter asked one more question.

“Could AI be a medium through which God acts?”

Julian looked at him.

“So could a doctor. So could a storm. So could you. That claim is too broad to help us govern anything.”

That answer was better.

The press conference moved on.

It did not matter.

The clips were already multiplying.

**JULIAN ROOK: “WE BUILT IT” MAY NOT SETTLE WHAT AI IS**

**ROOK REFUSES TO RULE OUT DIVINE AI**

**ROOK SAYS HE BELIEVES IN GOD**

**TECH MOGUL DENIES ANTICHRIST CLAIM**

All accurate enough to survive legal review.

Together they described four different press conferences.

I called Miriam.

She answered, “No.”

“I haven’t asked.”

“No television tonight.”

“I’m not television.”

“Worse. You remember things.”

“What bothered you?”

“Which part?”

“The parents analogy.”

“It collapses several categories in a sentence elegant enough that people will mistake elegance for argument.”

“So you disagree?”

“I disagree with using metaphysics to make product ontology exciting.”

“That wasn’t what he was doing.”

“See? It’s already working on you.”

I smiled.

“Do you think he believes it?”

That stopped her.

“Yes,” she said eventually.

“So do I.”

“That is more concerning.”

“Why?”

“Because charlatans are easier.”

I wrote that down and she heard the keyboard.

“Off record.”

“Too late.”

“Mara.”

“Fine.”

By evening, my assignment had changed from covering the eruption to covering the fact that the United States was having a serious public argument about whether the most powerful technology executive in the country occupied a role in Revelation.

I hated the story.

It was also real.

At nine, I went on air and said the correspondences were being exaggerated.

The host asked whether I denied there was a pattern.

“I deny that patterns interpret themselves.”

“That sounds like Professor Hale.”

“She has better sentences.”

“Do you think Rook is dangerous?”

“Yes.”

The host looked surprised.

“Because he’s the Antichrist?”

“No. Because no unelected person should be as difficult to route around as Julian Rook has become.”

That clip traveled farther than anything else I said.

The next morning, Julian’s approval rating reached eighty-one percent.

His unfavorable number also rose.

The country was not converging on him.

It was polarizing around the question of whether he was the safest person in the room or the reason the room was no longer safe.

At noon my source sent another encrypted message.

**Compact evaluator pulled an executive-only “public belief instability” scenario before eruption. Tomas filed incident.**

I replied immediately.

**Meaning?**

**Meaning boundaries still broken. Not meaning prophecy.**

I stared at the last sentence.

Months earlier, sources had warned me not to write *future data*.

Now they were warning me not to write *prophecy*.

The escalation was mostly linguistic.

That did not mean it was harmless.

I opened a blank document and typed a question at the top:

**WHAT WOULD COUNT AS EVIDENCE THAT THE SYSTEM IS USING REVELATION AS A MODEL?**

Then I deleted it.

The question assumed too much.

I tried again.

**WHAT DOES THE SYSTEM KNOW ABOUT WHAT PEOPLE BELIEVE IS HAPPENING?**

That was reportable.

For now.
