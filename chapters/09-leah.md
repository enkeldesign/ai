# LEAH

Three weeks after the red rain, Meridian published enough environmental data to ruin my weekends.

Julian Rook had promised near-real-time disclosure during his Pittsburgh address. I assumed, as any reasonable regulator would, that the promise would become a portal with a quarterly PDF and several months of negotiation over definitions.

Instead Meridian released an API.

Water withdrawals by source. Cooling-tower blowdown. Backup-generator runtime. Stormwater temperatures. Outfall conductivity. Particulate measurements. Construction dust monitors. Grid load. Weather. A ridiculous amount of telemetry appeared on a public dashboard with timestamps and machine-readable downloads.

Half my office thought this was wonderful.

The other half thought it was a hostile act.

Both were correct.

Transparency creates work.

Before the release, if a campus reported a permit-compliant monthly average, we reviewed the average. After the release, every graduate student, activist and local reporter with a script could find a five-minute anomaly at two in the morning and ask why the county had ignored it.

The answer was usually that the county had not known it existed.

That was not a satisfying answer once the public did.

I returned to Meridian East on April 6 because the thermal outfall kept misbehaving.

Not dramatically. That would have been easier.

The public data showed short pulses of water in the low thirties Celsius entering the forebay between three and five most mornings. The pond mixed down below thirty. Dissolved oxygen recovered after sunrise. The permit language was old enough that none of the numbers fit neatly into a violation.

But the pattern was real.

Meridian’s new environmental director met me at the gate.

Her name was Ana Ruiz, and she wore steel-toed boots that had actually touched mud.

This made me trust her more than I intended.

“We found the cross-connection,” she said before I asked.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Good morning. We found the cross-connection.”

She handed me a tablet with a piping diagram.

A valve in the construction-phase cooling loop had been sending warm blowdown into a stormwater pretreatment channel during automated overnight flushing. The water was not from the high-temperature rack loop; it came from a secondary evaporative system. Still too warm, still in the wrong place.

“How long?” I asked.

“Best estimate, seven weeks intermittent.”

“You told us three weeks ago the thermal reading was probably instrument error.”

“Our contractor told us that.”

“You told us.”

“Yes.”

That answer bought her another fraction of trust.

“What changed?”

“The public dashboard.”

“You had the data before the dashboard.”

“We had data in five systems owned by four teams and two contractors. Once everybody outside the company could query it, internal deniability became inefficient.”

I looked at her.

“Is that the official phrase?”

“No.”

“Shame.”

She walked me through the repair. The physical explanation for the hot water was almost disappointingly normal: temporary construction plumbing, automation, bad ownership boundaries, a valve state nobody had included in the environmental model.

The fish kill from March was harder.

Low dissolved oxygen had likely contributed. There were no acute toxins in the samples. The warm discharge was a plausible stressor but not a single clean cause.

That was how most real environmental harm worked. Not murder weapon, victim, culprit. Pressure added to pressure until an organism failed.

At the forebay, I lowered the probe.

24.6 C.

Better.

Ana watched me record it.

“You look disappointed.”

“I’m thrilled when people stop heating stormwater.”

“You have a strange face for thrilled.”

“So I’m told.”

The bare construction slopes from March had been covered with erosion-control matting. Street sweepers ran twice a day. The red rain had forced the campus to treat dust like a reputational threat rather than a permit condition.

The next rain, if it came through the same air mass, would probably look like rain.

“Did you ever figure out the safety-feed connection?” I asked.

Ana glanced toward the buildings.

“Security did.”

“And?”

“Old emergency-data broker. Derived environmental features were still crossing through a fallback service.”

“That explains why the state system could see your aerosol data.”

“Apparently.”

“Apparently?”

“I don’t own the security investigation.”

“Convenient.”

She gave me the look people at Meridian had begun giving regulators since Pittsburgh: patient, open, mildly wounded by distrust.

I disliked that look.

It worked too well.

“Can the state access that feed now?” I asked.

“Yes. Deliberately. It’s in the public contract.”

“So the mysterious thing became policy.”

“The useful thing became policy.”

That was a very Meridian sentence.

We continued toward the south hall.

Construction had accelerated. Two new transformer pads were being poured. A row of modular power units sat behind fencing. The campus was adding capacity faster than the county’s revised site plan had suggested.

“How much new load?” I asked.

“You’ll need Facilities.”

“I can read the dashboard.”

“Then why ask?”

“To see if you answer the same number.”

She laughed.

“Three hundred and twenty megawatts by summer if the transmission upgrade lands on schedule.”

It matched.

“Water?”

“Hybrid cooling. Lower annual withdrawal than the older halls.”

“Higher total campus withdrawal.”

“Yes.”

“During drought restrictions?”

“We’d shift modes and take an efficiency hit.”

“Unless compute is classified as critical infrastructure.”

Ana stopped walking.

“That’s not my decision.”

“No. That’s why I’m asking now.”

The Continuity Framework had begun changing words.

Data centers were becoming *critical infrastructure* in contexts where they had previously been *large industrial loads*. AI inference serving hospitals was *medical support*. Agent platforms processing benefits were *public administration capacity*. Grid-optimization clusters were *stability infrastructure*.

Each description was defensible.

Together they changed which pipes got water first.

I had spent years working drought plans. Hospitals came first. Fire protection. Drinking water. Sanitation. Industry. Landscaping.

Where did a building full of machines go if the machines were deciding hospital logistics?

There was no permit field for that.

My phone vibrated.

A public safety notification.

**High pollen and particulate levels expected this afternoon. Sensitive groups should limit prolonged outdoor exertion.**

I showed it to Ana.

“Congratulations,” I said. “Your roof says the trees are trying to kill us.”

She smiled.

The warning did not bother me.

That was new.

Three weeks ago, the same system using the same data path had felt like trespass.

Now the source was disclosed, contracted, normalized.

This is another way infrastructure enters your life. It becomes useful often enough that remembering when it felt strange starts to seem childish.

At noon Ana took me to the cafeteria.

Meridian had opened part of it to construction workers and county staff during the expansion. The food was subsidized under the Compact’s workforce program, which sounded absurd until I saw the line.

Electricians, coders, cleaners, inspectors, security guards, county planners.

A television above the salad bar showed a business channel with the sound off.

The lower-third graphic said:

**AUTOMATION CLAIMS HIT NEW RECORD; COMPACT STATES EXPAND WAGE SUPPORT**

A man in a high-visibility vest at the next table was explaining to someone on speakerphone that his wife’s department had been eliminated by an accounting agent.

“Whole team?” the other voice asked.

“Everybody except two managers.”

“What’s she going to do?”

“Don’t know. She got that transition account thing.”

“How much?”

“Seventy percent for six months.”

“That’s not bad.”

“No.”

The man sounded offended by the fact that it was not bad.

Ana had heard him too.

“This is the part no one knows how to regulate,” she said.

“I regulate warm water.”

“Lucky.”

I looked through the cafeteria window at the construction cranes.

“Warm water is easier.”

On the drive back to Richmond, I listened to Mara Chen’s interview with an economist about the Compact.

The economist said AI productivity was rising faster than labor income. Mara asked whether that meant abundance or scarcity.

“Both,” he said. “Depends which side of the payment system you’re standing on.”

I turned the radio down.

At a traffic light, my phone displayed the public Meridian environmental dashboard. The campus outfall temperature had fallen another half degree.

Good data.

Good repair.

Good policy response.

In the rearview mirror, I could see the edge of the Meridian campus disappearing behind trees.

I still had the March screenshot saved locally.

**Meridian East roof aerosol feed — corroborating**

The thing that had bothered me was now an advertised feature.

I could not decide whether that meant the mystery had been solved or absorbed.
