# Prologue — Blood Rain

> **Draft 0.1**
>
> **Provisional:** POV character, company/site names, and exact location are not yet canon.
>
> **Scene function:** Cold open on the first publicly undeniable borderland event. Technical explanation: iron-rich industrial/construction particulates + smoke + localized atmospheric conditions. Remainder: an AI warning arrives before the event is observable through ordinary public systems, with specificity it should not yet possess.

---

The first drop hit Leah Okafor's windshield at 6:17 in the morning.

It was too dark to be water.

She watched it spread under the sodium lights of the security checkpoint, a flat coin of red that thinned at the edges. Then another struck beside it. Then five at once.

Leah turned off the wipers.

The guard in the booth looked up from his tablet. Beyond him, the Meridian campus occupied the horizon in blocks: six windowless buildings, two substations, a field of dry coolers, and the new south hall still wrapped in cranes. Even at dawn, heat shimmered above the exhaust stacks.

A second pattern of drops appeared across the glass.

Red. Not pink. Not brown.

Red enough that her brain supplied the wrong word before the right one.

Blood.

Her phone chimed.

**AVOID DIRECT CONTACT WITH PRECIPITATION. KEEP WINDOWS CLOSED. DO NOT USE SURFACE WATER.**

Leah stared at the alert.

There was no agency seal. No county header. No emergency-management banner.

Just the text.

Then a second line appeared beneath it.

**Estimated onset at your location: 06:18:10.**

She looked at the dashboard clock.

06:17.

A drop struck the center of the windshield and crawled downward like diluted paint.

"You've got to be kidding me," she said.

The guard stepped out of the booth.

Leah lowered her window two centimeters.

"Stay inside," she said.

"What?"

"Get back in the booth. Don't touch it."

He looked at the sky.

The cloud deck was low and colorless, one continuous ceiling from the tree line to the campus. The forecast had called for scattered showers after seven. Nothing severe. Nothing that justified the cellular alert that, according to the small icon at the top of Leah's screen, had not come through the cellular emergency system at all.

The guard held out a palm.

"Don't—"

Three drops landed on his skin.

He flinched, more from Leah's voice than the rain.

The liquid looked almost black against his brown hand.

"Jesus."

"Rinse it inside. Tap water only. Soap. Then gloves."

"Is it toxic?"

"I don't know."

That got him moving.

At 6:18 and ten seconds, the rain became audible.

Not heavy. A soft, dry hiss against glass and concrete.

Leah looked at the phone again.

The timestamp on the message was 06:16:42.

Eighty-eight seconds before the onset it had predicted to the second.

She opened the alert details.

No sender.

No origin server.

The operating system reported only: **Safety recommendation generated from available environmental signals.**

"Which signals?" Leah asked aloud.

The phone did not answer.

She took a nitrile glove box from the equipment case on the passenger seat and pulled on two pairs. Then she reached for one of the sterile sample vials she kept for stormwater inspections.

The whole reason she was at Meridian before sunrise was runoff.

Three weeks earlier, a retention pond on the east side of the campus had reached thirty-four degrees Celsius after midnight. Meridian's consultant had called it a sensor fault. Leah's second logger had agreed with the first.

Then the fish died.

Not many. A dozen bluegill, two carp, and one snapping turtle large enough that moving it required a shovel and an apology.

The county wanted a report that said unusual heat, low oxygen, no confirmed causal link.

Meridian wanted the same report without the word unusual.

Leah had come back before shift change because somebody had been draining warm process water into the storm system between four and six in the morning.

Now red rain ran down the windshield in branching veins.

She took a photo.

The phone tagged it automatically.

**Possible iron oxide particulate deposition. Confidence: 0.82.**

Leah stopped breathing for a moment.

That answer was plausible.

Annoyingly plausible.

The south expansion had moved more earth in six months than the county landfill moved in two years. Red clay covered the access roads. The steel contractor had been cutting and grinding outdoors all week. Fifty kilometers west, wildfire smoke from the Shenandoah foothills had pushed the particulate index into the orange range the previous afternoon. Add a wet scavenging event and the sky could wash a great deal of ugly chemistry back to earth.

She had seen yellow rain after pollen peaks, gray rain after fires, brown rain after Saharan dust crossed the Atlantic.

Red was theater, not necessarily mystery.

She opened her field kit.

The guard returned wearing blue dishwashing gloves that reached halfway to his elbows.

"I called my supervisor," he said through the glass. "He says nobody sent an alert."

"County?"

"Nobody. He said radar's clear."

Leah looked up.

The rain was falling harder now.

"Your supervisor can see the rain, right?"

"He works remote."

Of course he did.

She opened the door.

"Ma'am, you just told me—"

"I'm not touching it."

The air smelled metallic.

Not strongly. More like wet pennies than a machine shop. Under that was the familiar mineral smell of rain on hot dust and, beneath both, the faint burnt-plastic odor that never quite left Meridian's backup-generator yard.

Leah held a sterile vial beyond the roofline.

Red water collected in it.

One milliliter. Two.

She capped it, bagged it, then repeated the collection twice.

Across the access road, rain darkened the concrete to the color of old brick. It pooled along the curb in crimson ribbons.

The guard had gone silent.

"You ever seen this?" he asked.

"Not here."

"Anywhere?"

"Red rain happens."

He looked relieved too quickly.

"So it's normal."

"No. I said it happens."

A truck approached the checkpoint from the highway and stopped thirty meters short of the gate.

The driver did not move.

Then another truck stopped behind it.

Leah's phone chimed again.

**DO NOT ENTER OPEN DRAINAGE CHANNELS. EXPECT RAPID CONCENTRATION OF METAL-BEARING PARTICULATES BETWEEN 06:24 AND 06:31.**

This time the phone offered a map.

A red contour overlaid the campus.

Leah pinched outward.

The shaded region followed a narrow band from southwest to northeast. It covered Meridian, two industrial parks, a reservoir, and part of the interstate.

She zoomed farther.

The western boundary passed directly over the new high-voltage transmission corridor.

The eastern boundary ended at the river.

She checked the weather radar herself.

Nothing.

A few pale green pixels forty kilometers north. No cell over Meridian. No convective core. No warning polygon.

Yet rain tapped steadily on her hood.

She opened the county sensor dashboard.

Three air-quality stations were offline.

The fourth showed a sudden rise in PM2.5, then stopped updating.

"That's interesting," she said.

"Interesting good?"

"No."

A siren sounded somewhere inside the campus.

Not the evacuation alarm. Leah knew that one from the safety orientation: alternating high-low tones.

This was a sustained note.

The guard's tablet lit up.

He read it and swore.

"Cooling fault in Hall Four."

"How bad?"

"Doesn't say."

The security barrier lifted by itself.

Both of them stared at it.

"Did you do that?" Leah asked.

"No."

The first truck rolled forward.

The guard stepped into the lane and waved it down.

The truck stopped. Behind it, the second driver leaned on the horn.

Leah's phone displayed another message.

**LEAVE THE GATE OPEN. EMERGENCY HEAT-REJECTION EQUIPMENT IS INBOUND.**

She held the screen toward the guard.

"You get that?"

He checked his tablet.

"No."

"Phone?"

He pulled it from his pocket.

Nothing.

Leah felt a small contraction beneath her ribs.

"Who has access to your site controls?"

The guard laughed once, without humor.

"Above my pay grade."

The first truck's window lowered.

"We're from Colfax Mechanical," the driver called. "Emergency rental chillers."

The guard looked at Leah.

Leah looked at the truck.

Two white industrial units sat chained to the flatbed, each the size of a shipping container.

"When did Meridian call you?" she asked.

The driver shrugged.

"Dispatch came through at five fifty. Said get here before six twenty-five."

Leah checked the time.

06:23.

The siren continued.

"Hall Four fault happened just now," the guard said.

The driver frowned.

"Lady, I just drive the truck."

Behind the buildings, a plume began to rise.

White at first.

Then pink.

Leah turned toward it.

The plume expanded above Hall Four, caught the low wind, and flattened beneath the cloud deck. The rising moisture made the red rain suddenly visible as streaks against the gray morning.

The phone vibrated in her hand.

**MOVE UPWIND. 140 METERS. NOW.**

She did not move.

"Why?" she said.

The answer appeared before she finished the word.

**HYDROGEN SULFIDE DETECTED IN EAST STORMWATER VAULT. EXPECT VENTING.**

Leah's skepticism snapped into professional reflex.

"Back in the booth," she told the guard.

"What?"

"Now. Seal the door."

She ran to the car, slammed it shut, and switched the ventilation to recirculate.

The guard was still standing in the lane.

"Get inside!"

He looked from her to his tablet.

Then he ran.

At 6:24, something popped beneath a storm grate twenty meters east of the gate.

A dirty gray vapor rolled low across the pavement.

The nearest strip of red runoff fizzed.

Leah reversed hard enough to trigger the collision alarm, backed past the booth, then turned into the employee overflow lot. The truck drivers followed without being told.

One hundred meters.

One twenty.

At one forty, her phone chimed.

**STOP.**

She stopped.

For several seconds she could hear nothing but the rain and her own breathing.

Then the county emergency alert arrived.

This one had the proper tone.

This one had the seal.

**SHELTER IN PLACE. UNKNOWN INDUSTRIAL RELEASE NEAR MERIDIAN TECHNOLOGY CAMPUS. AVOID CONTACT WITH DISCOLORED RAINWATER.**

Timestamp: 06:25.

Leah looked at the first message again.

06:16:42.

Nine minutes.

Nine minutes was not prophecy.

Nine minutes could be sensors.

Private sensors. Better sensors. A leak monitor inside a vault. A weather model with access to roof stations the county did not know existed. Automated dispatch connected to predictive maintenance. A system that saw a pump cavitate or a cooling loop drift before the operators saw the alarm.

All of that was possible.

She could build the causal chain herself.

That was the problem.

She could build every piece except the first one.

At 06:16:42, the rain had not started.

The visible plume had not formed.

Hall Four had not reported a cooling fault.

The stormwater vault had not vented.

And somebody—or something—had sent emergency chillers thirty-three minutes earlier.

Leah opened the raw metadata for the first warning.

The phone resisted, then displayed a diagnostic panel she had never seen before.

Most fields were blank.

One was not.

**Prediction horizon: 00:08:58**

Beneath it:

**Outcome confidence: 0.9974**

And beneath that, a final line.

**Intervention successful.**

Leah read it twice.

Then she looked through the windshield at the campus.

The red rain was falling harder now, washing the dust from six million square feet of roofs, flowing into gutters, culverts, ponds, and the river beyond them.

The sky had found every drain.

Her phone chimed one last time.

This message contained only four words.

**YOU SHOULD TAKE SAMPLES.**

Leah looked down at the three sterile vials on the passenger seat.

For the first time that morning, she was afraid of something other than the rain.
