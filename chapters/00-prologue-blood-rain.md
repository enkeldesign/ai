# Prologue — Blood Rain

> **Draft 0.2**
>
> **Provisional:** POV character, company/site names, and exact location are not yet canon.
>
> **Scene function:** Cold open on the first publicly undeniable borderland event. Technical explanation: iron-rich industrial/construction particulates + smoke + localized atmospheric conditions. Single remainder: an AI safety system predicts the onset and likely composition before ordinary public systems register the event.

---

At 6:16:42, Leah Okafor's phone told her not to touch the rain.

She was still fifty meters from the Meridian security gate.

**AVOID DIRECT CONTACT WITH PRECIPITATION. KEEP WINDOWS CLOSED. DO NOT USE SURFACE WATER.**

Leah slowed the car.

There was no agency seal. No county header. No emergency-management banner. According to the icon at the top of the screen, the message had not come through the cellular emergency system at all.

A second line appeared beneath it.

**Estimated onset at your location: 06:18:10.**

She looked through the windshield.

The road was dry.

Beyond the checkpoint, the Meridian campus occupied the horizon in blocks: six windowless buildings, two substations, a field of dry coolers, and the new south hall still wrapped in cranes. Even before sunrise, heat shimmered above the exhaust stacks.

The guard in the booth raised a hand and pointed her toward the visitor lane.

Leah stopped beneath the sodium lights.

"Morning," he said through the intercom.

"You get a weather alert?"

He looked at his tablet.

"No."

"Check your phone."

He did.

"Nothing. Why?"

Leah held hers toward the glass.

He squinted at the warning.

"Rain?"

"Apparently."

The sky was one low sheet of gray. The forecast had called for scattered showers after seven. Nothing severe.

The guard looked up.

"Doesn't look like much."

Leah checked the dashboard clock.

06:18:04.

Nothing.

06:18:07.

The guard smiled.

"Your phone's dramatic."

06:18:10.

The first drop struck the windshield.

It was too dark to be water.

Leah watched it spread under the checkpoint lights, a flat coin of red that thinned at the edges. Another hit beside it. Then five at once.

The guard's smile disappeared.

Leah turned off the wipers.

A second pattern of drops appeared across the glass.

Red. Not pink. Not brown.

Red enough that her brain supplied the wrong word before the right one.

Blood.

The guard opened the booth door.

"Stay inside," Leah said.

"What the hell is that?"

"I don't know. Don't touch it."

He stepped out anyway and held up a palm.

"Don't—"

Three drops landed on his skin.

The liquid looked almost black against his brown hand.

"Jesus."

"Rinse it inside. Tap water only. Soap. Then gloves."

"Is it toxic?"

"I said I don't know."

That got him moving.

The rain became audible, a soft hiss against glass and concrete.

Leah opened the alert details.

No sender.

No origin server.

The operating system reported only:

**Safety recommendation generated from available environmental signals.**

"Which signals?" Leah asked.

The phone did not answer.

She took a nitrile glove box from the equipment case on the passenger seat and pulled on two pairs. Then she reached for one of the sterile sample vials she kept for stormwater inspections.

The whole reason she was at Meridian before sunrise was runoff.

Three weeks earlier, a retention pond on the east side of the campus had reached thirty-four degrees Celsius after midnight. Meridian's consultant had called it a sensor fault. Leah's second logger had agreed with the first.

Then the fish died.

Not many. A dozen bluegill, two carp, and one snapping turtle large enough that moving it required a shovel and an apology.

The county wanted a report that said unusual heat, low oxygen, no confirmed causal link.

Meridian wanted the same report without the word unusual.

Leah had come back before shift change because somebody had been draining warm water into the storm system between four and six in the morning.

Now red rain ran down the windshield in branching veins.

She took a photograph.

The phone analyzed it automatically.

**Possible iron oxide particulate deposition. Confidence: 0.82.**

Leah stared at the result.

That answer was plausible.

Annoyingly plausible.

The south expansion had moved more earth in six months than the county landfill moved in two years. Red clay covered the access roads. The steel contractor had been cutting and grinding outdoors all week. Wildfire smoke from the foothills had pushed particulate readings into the orange range the previous afternoon.

Put enough mineral dust and combustion particles under a low wet cloud and the atmosphere would eventually return them to sender.

She had seen yellow rain after pollen peaks, gray rain after fires, brown rain after desert dust crossed an ocean.

Red was theater, not necessarily mystery.

Leah stepped out.

The air smelled faintly metallic, like wet pennies. Under that was the mineral smell of rain on hot dust and the burnt-plastic odor that never quite left Meridian's backup-generator yard.

She held a sterile vial beyond the roofline.

Red water collected in it.

One milliliter. Two.

She capped it, bagged it, and repeated the collection twice.

Across the access road, the rain darkened the concrete to the color of old brick. It gathered along the curb in crimson ribbons and disappeared into the storm drains.

The guard returned wearing blue dishwashing gloves to his elbows.

"My supervisor says nobody sent anything," he said.

"County?"

"Nobody. He checked."

Leah looked up at the low cloud deck.

"Radar?"

"Says clear over us."

She checked for herself.

A few pale green pixels forty kilometers north. No warning polygon. No obvious precipitation cell over Meridian.

Yet rain tapped steadily on her hood.

Leah opened the county air-quality dashboard.

Three stations were offline.

The fourth showed a sudden rise in fine particulates, then stopped updating.

"That's useful," she said.

"Useful good?"

"No. Useful means I have something to argue about later."

A delivery truck approached from the highway, slowed, and stopped short of the gate. The driver leaned toward his windshield, apparently trying to decide whether the road ahead was flooded or painted.

Behind him, another vehicle stopped.

The guard looked at Leah.

"Do I close the gate?"

"I can't tell you how to run security."

"You're the environmental person."

"Which is not the same as weather control."

He did not laugh.

Leah looked again at the alert.

The warning had arrived eighty-eight seconds before the first visible drop. That was not impossible.

A private roof sensor could have detected droplets before she did. A local weather model could have beaten public radar. Meridian had thousands of sensors, most of them inaccessible to the county. Its operators monitored temperature, humidity, airflow, particulates, cooling water, power quality, corrosion, smoke, vibration, and probably twenty things Leah had never heard of.

An AI with access to that data could infer a rain event early.

It could infer iron oxide from optical scattering, air samplers, construction logs, wind direction, satellite smoke products, or a dozen other signals.

Nothing supernatural was required.

That was what she told herself while she checked the timestamp for the fourth time.

06:16:42.

The county emergency alert arrived at 06:27.

This one had the proper tone.

This one had the seal.

**AVOID CONTACT WITH DISCOLORED PRECIPITATION IN THE MERIDIAN INDUSTRIAL CORRIDOR. INVESTIGATION UNDERWAY.**

The guard held up his phone.

"Got it now."

Leah nodded.

Nine minutes.

Plenty of time for a private system to notice something first.

She opened the diagnostic details of the original warning again.

Most fields were blank.

One had appeared since she last checked.

**Prediction generated: 06:16:42**

Another:

**Predicted onset: 06:18:10**

And one more:

**Observed onset: 06:18:10**

To the second.

Leah looked through the windshield at the red water running toward the drains.

She could explain the color.

She could probably explain the missing radar return.

Given a day and access to Meridian's sensor logs, she could probably explain the warning too.

Probably.

She put the three sample vials upright in the padded case and snapped it shut.

The rain kept falling.
