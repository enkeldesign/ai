# MIRIAM

The church food pantry stopped asking people to prove they were poor in June.

That was the most theologically significant decision I saw all summer.

Before then, visitors filled out a form. Household income. Address. Number of dependents. Employment status. The questions existed because grants required documentation and because institutions become nervous when generosity cannot be audited.

Then half the people in line began carrying phones that already knew more about their finances than the form did.

A volunteer suggested connecting the pantry to the federal support system so eligibility could be verified automatically.

The pastor refused.

“If they came here,” she said, “they’re eligible.”

Somebody pointed out that people might take food they did not need.

The pastor said, “Then God will have to survive the fraud.”

I was there because the divinity school had started coordinating churches that were suddenly feeding twice as many households as the previous year.

This was not my specialty.

Apocalyptic literature does not qualify a person to manage refrigerated storage.

But academics are more useful during crises when someone ignores what they are experts in.

I spent the morning carrying boxes of beans and listening to people explain why they were embarrassed.

A paralegal whose firm had automated document review.

A customer-service manager whose entire team had been replaced by agents she had helped train.

A teacher whose husband still had a job but whose home insurance premium had doubled.

A retired couple whose pension was intact but whose adult children had moved back in.

Nobody described themselves as poor.

They described a before and an after.

At noon Ezekiel Boone arrived with three vans of food.

I knew he was coming and had considered leaving.

That would have been childish.

Zeke had spent the previous month telling millions of viewers that the economic order was beginning to resemble Revelation’s image of controlled buying and selling. He was careful enough to say resemblance rather than fulfillment when interviewed by serious outlets and less careful on his own streams.

I had criticized him publicly.

He had responded by recommending my book.

This was infuriatingly effective.

He came through the loading door carrying a carton of canned tomatoes.

“Professor Hale.”

“Mr. Boone.”

“You can call me Zeke.”

“I know.”

He put the box down.

“You still think I’m reckless?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I was worried television had softened you.”

I pointed toward another pallet.

“If you want theological combat, carry rice.”

He did.

For an hour we worked without discussing Revelation.

That improved both of us.

Zeke knew the pantry network better than I did. His organization had started bulk-buying shelf-stable food months earlier, which would have looked like paranoid hoarding if shortages had not arrived. Now his warehouses supplied churches, mosques, community centers and secular mutual-aid groups across four states.

“You know your audience thinks the Mark is coming,” I said while we stacked bags.

“Some of them think it’s already here.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

I stopped.

He smiled.

“You look disappointed.”

“I keep being promised a fanatic.”

“I have days.”

“What would count as the Mark?”

He leaned against the pallet.

“Not a chip.”

“Thank God.”

“Literally?”

“Don’t.”

He laughed.

Then he became serious.

“The text says people cannot buy or sell without it. Christians turned that into a technology guessing game because gadgets are easier to fear than allegiance.”

“That is closer to my reading.”

“I know.”

“Then why tell people the current system resembles it?”

“Because it does.”

“No. It resembles the economic function if you choose that frame.”

“Yes.”

“That qualification is the entire argument.”

Zeke picked up another bag of rice.

“Professor, do you think people in line care whether their dependency is typologically exact?”

“That is not a sentence a responsible interpreter gets to say.”

“It is if irresponsible interpreters are the only ones naming what dependency feels like.”

I disliked that because it contained a real criticism.

Academics are often late to moral language. We wait for distinctions to stabilize while other people are being injured by the thing we are distinguishing.

But speed creates its own injuries.

A volunteer interrupted us to ask whether a family could receive two protein boxes because they were caring for an elderly parent.

“Of course,” I said.

Zeke waited until she left.

“You just allocated by need.”

“I gave them beans.”

“That is how allocation begins.”

“Please do not make lentils eschatological.”

“I make no promises.”

In the afternoon a federal benefits agent went down for forty minutes.

The pantry line grew because two nearby grocery stores had begun limiting purchases for accounts whose emergency food credits could not be verified.

The policy was intended to prevent fraud during the outage.

It also meant people with government support could not use the support while the verification service was unavailable.

Our pantry had no such problem.

We handed out food.

A mother with two boys started crying when the pastor told her there was no form.

Not dramatic crying. The exhausted kind that embarrassed her more because it would not stop.

Zeke carried her groceries to the car.

I watched from the doorway.

There is a temptation in theology to assume the person with the worse doctrine will also have the worse character.

History discourages that assumption.

After closing, Zeke and I sat on the church steps with paper cups of coffee.

“You know why I named my son Daniel?” he asked.

“I didn’t know you had a son.”

“He’s nineteen.”

“Because of the prophet?”

“Because my wife liked the name.”

I laughed.

“Disappointing.”

“Most biblical symbolism is discovered after the baby is born.”

That was good enough that I wrote it down.

He saw me.

“You’re going to steal that.”

“I cite my sources.”

His expression changed.

“My son thinks I’m hurting people.”

“With your broadcasts?”

“He thinks I make frightened Christians more frightened.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

The honesty surprised me.

“Then stop.”

“I also make some of them less afraid. If they believe the world has a shape, they act differently.”

“Sometimes worse.”

“Sometimes better.”

“Meaning?”

He nodded toward the pantry.

“Half those vans were paid for by people who think the end of the age might be close.”

“Charity does not validate eschatology.”

“I know.”

“Then why say it?”

“Because consequences matter even when they don’t prove truth.”

That was another thing I wished he had said badly.

The sun had gone orange through wildfire haze from hundreds of miles away.

For several minutes neither of us spoke.

Then Zeke asked, “Have you watched Rook lately?”

“Yes.”

“What do you think?”

“I think people want me to say something more interesting than the evidence allows.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

I looked at him.

“What was?”

“Do you trust him?”

“No.”

“Do you distrust him?”

“No.”

He smiled.

“You and the government guy would get along.”

“Daniel Kade?”

“You know him?”

“Only from television.”

“He said almost the same thing.”

I looked down at my coffee.

That annoyed me more than it should have.

Zeke continued.

“I don’t think Rook is the interesting part.”

“What is?”

“The way people talk about the system when it saves them.”

“Gratitude?”

“More than gratitude.”

He searched for the word.

“Providence.”

I felt the argument arrive before he made it.

“No.”

“You haven’t heard it?”

“I’ve heard people use religious language metaphorically about technology since before you had a channel.”

“I’m not talking about slogans.”

He took out his phone and played me a clip from a woman in Texas whose neighborhood had kept power during the heat emergency because the allocation system shifted load around a medical cooling center.

She was standing in her kitchen.

“I prayed,” the woman said, “and then the notice came saying our block would stay on because my mother’s ventilator was registered. Tell me God didn’t answer through that thing.”

Zeke stopped the video.

“She knows the technical explanation,” he said. “She does not think the electrons are magic.”

“One person using providential language does not establish a theological trend.”

“I have six thousand clips.”

Of course he did.

I stared at the dark phone screen.

The theological problem was not whether the system was God.

That question was still absurdly premature.

The problem was that the system had begun occupying the social space where people expected providence to appear.

Food.

Power.

Medicine.

Warning.

Rescue.

Judgment.

“I still think you’re reckless,” I said.

Zeke stood and picked up our empty cups.

“I was counting on it.”

He threw them away.

Before leaving, he turned back.

“When you finally get scared, call me.”

“I am scared now.”

That stopped him.

“Of Revelation?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then what?”

I looked through the church doors at the empty tables where hundreds of people had collected food without proving they deserved it.

“Of how quickly we are learning to mistake effective care for legitimate authority.”

Zeke nodded slowly.

“Close enough,” he said.

It was not.

That was why I remembered it.
