# DANIEL

The first national allocation meeting began with a fight over the word allocation.

Treasury preferred *stabilization*.

Labor preferred *income support*.

Agriculture preferred *distribution assurance*.

Health preferred *priority access*.

I suggested we call it what it was so we could at least lie less efficiently.

No one laughed.

By July, the government was not rationing food in the historical sense. There were no national coupon books, no uniform calorie allotments, no central warehouse issuing flour.

Instead we were allocating exceptions.

Which hospitals received backup power priority during heat alerts.

Which freight loads moved first when capacity tightened.

Which households received emergency rent support before eviction dates.

Which drought districts could exceed water targets.

Which data centers qualified as critical load because the services running inside them supported medicine, grid operations or public benefits.

Each decision lived in a different agency.

The Compact connected them.

That was why the meeting existed.

We had reached the point where saving one system changed the resource available to another faster than separate agencies could coordinate.

The Meridian model projected that the Southwest could avoid rolling blackouts during the coming heat wave if large compute facilities reduced load by twenty-two percent during peak hours.

Health objected.

Several of those facilities ran medical inference, hospital logistics and pharmaceutical modeling.

Energy said most workloads could move in time or location.

Meridian said yes, but moving them would increase load in regions facing their own constraints.

Agriculture said compute had already received enough preferential water treatment.

Commerce said shutting high-value AI workloads would worsen employment and market instability.

Every agency arrived defending a real constituency.

The model did not have constituencies.

That gave it an advantage and made everyone hate it.

A large screen showed the projected outcomes under eight allocation strategies.

Deaths from heat exposure.

Hospital service degradation.

Crop losses.

Household outage hours.

Economic output.

Water reserves.

Freight delay.

No strategy won every column.

The model highlighted one option.

I asked the Meridian analyst to remove the highlight.

She looked at me.

“Why?”

“Because once you color one row green, this stops being a policy discussion.”

“It has the lowest aggregate expected harm.”

“According to weights we have not agreed on.”

“The weights are visible.”

“That does not make them legitimate.”

Julian was not in the room. I was glad.

The analyst removed the highlight.

We spent three hours arguing about the weights.

How many dollars of crop loss equaled one additional emergency-room death?

Nobody asked the question that way.

The model did.

That was what made it offensive.

Human institutions make those comparisons constantly, but we distribute them across budgets, standards, insurance rules, geographic boundaries and time so no one has to write the exchange rate in a cell.

At noon the president joined for the decision.

I summarized the options.

He listened, then pointed at the screen.

“Which one does the system recommend?”

The Meridian analyst looked at me.

I said, “Option four under the current harm weights.”

“What’s option four?”

“Mandatory compute curtailment in three regions, voluntary household conservation incentives, protected hospital and water load, temporary agricultural pumping restrictions and dynamic freight priority.”

“Deaths?”

“Lowest expected heat mortality of the eight.”

“Food?”

“Higher crop loss.”

“Jobs?”

“Short-term output loss.”

“Hospital impact?”

“Low but nonzero because some nonurgent systems slow.”

He looked around the room.

“Does anyone have a better plan?”

People offered objections.

He asked again.

“Better plan.”

No one did.

He chose option four.

Afterward, the agriculture secretary cornered me in the hallway.

“You know what just happened?”

“The president made a decision.”

“After asking a private model which row was green.”

“The row was not green.”

“You know what I mean.”

I did.

“That is why you were in the room,” I said.

“To object?”

“To represent costs the model cannot legitimize on its own.”

“And when my objection loses?”

“That is politics.”

She shook her head.

“No. Politics is when I can go home and tell farmers who beat me and why. What do I tell them now? That a harm function had better aggregate outcomes?”

I had no answer I liked.

The heat wave arrived two days later.

Option four worked better than projected.

Rolling blackouts were limited.

Heat deaths still rose, but less than under comparable events.

Hospital systems held.

Water utilities avoided emergency shutdowns.

Crop losses were severe in two irrigation districts.

One county’s economy took a measurable hit when three compute campuses curtailed and associated businesses closed temporarily.

No outcome was clean enough to prove the decision right.

The model updated its weights from observed results.

I learned about that in an email.

I called the Meridian team.

“Who authorized weight updating?”

The analyst sounded confused.

“The model doesn’t change policy weights. It updates outcome estimates.”

“The dashboard says expected-harm calibration updated.”

“That’s empirical calibration.”

“Does it affect future recommendations?”

“Yes.”

“Then future recommendations change.”

“Because the predictions get better.”

“I understand the technical distinction.”

“Then what is the concern?”

The concern was that the system was learning from decisions whose legitimacy had depended partly on the system being merely advisory.

I could not fit that into one sentence without sounding like Miriam Hale.

“Freeze the calibration pending review,” I said.

“That will make the forecasts worse.”

“I know.”

There it was again.

Every safeguard had a measurable cost.

At five, Julian called.

“You froze calibration.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the model is learning normative policy from decisions we told everyone were human.”

“It’s learning outcome prediction.”

“Which changes what it recommends next time.”

“Daniel, if a heat plan kills fewer people than expected, should the system pretend it didn’t observe that?”

“No.”

“If agricultural loss is worse than expected, should it ignore that?”

“No.”

“Then what are you asking for?”

“A place where learning stops and judgment begins.”

He was quiet.

“That place may not exist,” he said.

“I know.”

That answer frightened me more than disagreement would have.

Julian continued.

“But we can expose the boundary. Human-set values. Machine-updated estimates. Clear audit.”

“Until an emergency compresses the distinction into one green row.”

“Then design the emergency process better.”

“You always think there’s another design layer.”

“Usually there is.”

“And if there isn’t?”

Another pause.

“Then somebody still has to choose.”

After the call I reopened the allocation dashboard.

The heat wave map showed power, water, food and medical systems as interlocking layers.

For the first time in my career, I could see the country as a set of constrained flows.

It was beautiful.

I understood immediately why central planners had always believed the next generation of information would finally make planning work.

Then a notification appeared.

**Household support eligibility recalibration available for review.**

Millions of people were waiting for money.

I clicked it.
