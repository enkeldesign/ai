# MARA

By eight that morning I had six videos of red rain, three mutually incompatible explanations, a county statement written by somebody who had been taught to fear nouns, and an editor asking whether the story was “weather weird” or “AI weird.”

I told him I would let him know when reality picked a desk.

The first clip had arrived at 6:41 from a security guard at Meridian East. He had filmed from beneath a gate canopy. The image quality was bad and the commentary worse, but the red water collecting at the edge of the pavement was clear enough.

The second came from a woman driving to work who had stopped her car to record the hood. She said, “This is literally blood,” which guaranteed the clip would travel farther than anything careful I could write.

The third was more useful. A construction foreman held a white five-gallon bucket under the rain for less than a minute. The water in the bottom looked like weak brick-colored tea. He had also recorded his phone screen, including a state safety advisory warning of potentially contaminated precipitation.

Timestamp: 6:14.

Rain visible in his video: 6:21.

Seven minutes did not make a miracle. Weather apps predicted rain every day. Local industrial sensors could detect particles before a person could see them. A machine-generated warning could combine signals faster than an agency could issue a statement.

The interesting question was not whether software had guessed correctly.

The interesting question was whose data it had used.

I called the state emergency communications office. The press officer said the advisory had not been manually issued by the state.

“Then why did it have the state icon?” I asked.

“The device safety framework uses approved state branding for certain geographically verified recommendations.”

“Recommendations generated by whom?”

“By the framework.”

“That’s not a who.”

“It isn’t intended to be.”

I wrote that down.

The press officer put me on hold for four minutes and came back with a sentence I recognized as having survived a lawyer.

“The advisory was generated from licensed and public environmental data sources under existing emergency-information agreements.”

“Did those sources include Meridian?”

“I don’t have visibility into individual source attribution.”

“Who does?”

“I can send you the public documentation.”

“Who does?”

“I’ll have to get back to you.”

People often accuse reporters of asking the same question until someone slips. That is sometimes what we are doing. More often, we are waiting to see whether the shape of the refusal changes.

This one had.

At 7:32 I called Meridian.

Their communications system recognized my number and routed me directly to a human being, which was either courtesy or threat assessment.

A press representative named Owen told me the precipitation appeared to be a regional atmospheric event unrelated to campus operations.

“How do you know that?”

“Preliminary environmental data.”

“Your data?”

“Among other sources.”

“Did Meridian provide environmental sensor data to the state device-safety framework this morning?”

“I’d have to check the details of our data-sharing agreements.”

“Yesterday your environmental counsel told the county that facility telemetry was segmented and unavailable externally without formal request.”

There was a silence just long enough to tell me he had not expected the sentence.

“Those are different systems,” he said.

“Which systems?”

“I don’t want to speculate.”

“Good. Neither do I.”

I asked for the technical contact responsible for environmental data exchange. He offered to send a statement.

We both knew he had won that round.

At 7:48 my editor, Nina, appeared in the glass doorway of the conference room where I had taken over a wall display.

“You’re trending behind a guy who says it’s one of the plagues.”

“Wrong book.”

“Does the internet care?”

“No.”

She came in carrying coffee and set one beside me.

On the wall I had six columns: **confirmed**, **likely**, **possible**, **claimed**, **unknown**, **bullshit**.

Nina pointed to the last one.

“Technically an editorial judgment.”

“It’s an internal working category.”

“What’s in it?”

“Nanobots. Chemtrails. Chinese weather weapons. Divine blood. A weapons test that uses ferric nanoparticles to map human movement.”

“Specific.”

“Conspiracy theories are usually more detailed than reporting.”

She looked at the confirmed column.

“Can we publish?”

“Red particulate rain happened. State safety software warned some users before it started. County says elevated particulate matter. Cause unknown. Meridian had local sensors in the affected area. We don’t know whether those sensors fed the warning.”

“That’s enough.”

“For a short.”

“For a short.”

I had already written the first version. The language was deliberately boring.

**Residents near a major Virginia data-center complex reported reddish precipitation early Thursday, prompting a precautionary automated safety advisory and a county investigation into elevated airborne particulates. Officials said there was no evidence the discoloration was biological.**

No blood in the headline.

No apocalypse.

No Julian Rook.

Nina read it over my shoulder.

“You’re leaving Meridian out of the headline?”

“Until we can connect it causally.”

“They’re in the lede.”

“Geographically.”

“You know every other outlet is going to say BLOOD RAIN AT ROOK AI CAMPUS.”

“Yes.”

“You’re comfortable losing traffic?”

“No. I’m comfortable losing a race to be wrong.”

She nodded once. That was why I worked for her.

We published at 8:06.

At 8:11 someone clipped my paragraph about mineral dust, removed the sentence saying the cause remained unknown, and posted it under the caption **MEDIA ALREADY COVERING FOR MERIDIAN**.

At 8:17 Meridian’s official account linked our story as evidence that “early reports are consistent with a nonhazardous mineral-aerosol event.”

At 8:20 I swore loudly enough that Nina shut my office door.

The problem with being careful in public is that anyone can use your caution as certainty.

I called Leah Okafor again.

She did not answer.

I sent a text instead.

**I’m not asking you to speculate. I need to know whether the safety advisory listed Meridian as a data source. Off record unless you say otherwise.**

Then I started on the background I should have been doing before the rain fell.

Meridian had spent four years becoming difficult to describe.

It was still called a technology company, but that was increasingly like calling a railroad a metalworking concern. Meridian trained models, operated cloud infrastructure, sold enterprise agents, managed power contracts, ran medical-discovery partnerships, built data centers, financed transmission projects and supplied optimization software to state agencies.

Julian Rook had a gift for making this sound less like concentration of power than administrative tidiness.

He was forty-six, tall, camera-friendly without looking manufactured, and famous for answering hostile questions as if the person asking had reminded him of something important. I had interviewed him twice. Both times I left with the unpleasant sensation that I had been treated better than I deserved.

The first interview had been about energy use. I had come prepared to challenge him on a new cluster of gas-backed data centers. He agreed with my strongest criticism before I made it, gave me better numbers than the public filings, and spent twenty minutes describing grid reforms I mostly supported.

Three weeks later Meridian announced a transmission partnership that turned part of my criticism into yesterday’s problem.

The second interview was about labor displacement.

I asked whether his company was destroying white-collar work faster than society could adapt.

He said yes.

Not *transforming*. Not *augmenting*. Yes.

Then he laid out a wage-insurance proposal more generous than the one being discussed in Congress and said, “If we make intelligence cheap and leave dignity expensive, that’s not a technological failure. That’s a political choice.”

The clip had been watched eighty million times.

I hated that it was a good answer.

At 9:03 an encrypted message arrived from a source at Meridian.

No greeting.

**You’re asking wrong question. Data access is weird but not main thing. Ask when safety model generated recommendation vs when source became available.**

I stared at the message.

The source had given me reliable infrastructure information twice before and had never sent anything remotely dramatic.

I typed:

**What source?**

Three dots appeared, vanished, reappeared.

**Roof aerosol feed among them. Don’t have enough to explain. Do not write “future data.” That’s not what I’m saying.**

I smiled despite myself.

The fastest way to make a reporter interested in a phrase is to tell her not to use it.

**Then what are you saying?**

No answer.

At 9:19 Nina forwarded a calendar alert from the political desk.

**JULIAN ROOK — NATIONAL ADDRESS, SATURDAY 7 PM ET**

The note beneath it said Meridian had booked simultaneous broadcast windows across all major platforms. The subject was “infrastructure continuity, AI transition, and national resilience.”

“National address?” I said aloud.

Nina leaned out of her office.

“He’s not president.”

“I noticed.”

“Political says three governors are expected to appear with him.”

“Which party?”

“Both kinds.”

That was more interesting than the red rain.

I pulled up Julian’s public schedule. The address had been added twenty-six minutes earlier.

His last scheduled event for Saturday had been a product keynote in San Jose.

Canceled.

I called Meridian communications again.

Owen answered this time with a sigh.

“Mara.”

“Owen.”

“You published a fair piece.”

“Don’t ruin it for me.”

He laughed, which I resented because it worked.

“What’s the address?” I asked.

“Julian believes the country is entering a period where infrastructure decisions can’t be separated from AI deployment.”

“That sounds like something Julian believes every day.”

“This is broader.”

“Is he running for office?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly.

“Was that ‘no’ approved?”

“Mara.”

“Is Meridian going to propose taking operational control of public infrastructure?”

“Absolutely not.”

“Providing software that makes the operational decisions?”

“We already provide software to utilities and agencies.”

“At what scale?”

“You’ll have to watch Saturday.”

I looked at the videos still cycling on the wall.

Red water on white concrete.

A warning generated before anyone official seemed to know what was happening.

A private sensor that might have been inside the system producing the warning.

None of it proved anything about Julian Rook.

That was precisely why I did not like the way my mind was arranging the facts.

At 10:07 Leah finally replied.

She sent one image.

A screenshot from the safety advisory.

Under **Signal sources** it listed regional monitors, satellite products, local precipitation sensors and, at the bottom:

**Meridian East roof aerosol feed — corroborating**

Her message contained five words.

**This is what bothers me.**

I called her immediately.

She declined the call.

Then another message arrived.

**Not for publication yet. I need to verify their architecture.**

That was fair.

I moved *Meridian sensor in advisory* from **possible** to **confirmed privately**.

Then I added a new line under **unknown**.

**Who connected it?**

At 10:30 the red-rain story dropped out of the top five on our traffic dashboard.

Markets had opened lower after another wave of automated layoffs.

A hospital network had announced that its diagnostic AI would begin handling first-line triage for sixteen million patients.

Two states were threatening legal action over Meridian’s new power contracts.

And Julian Rook, who did not hold office, was preparing to address the nation.

By lunchtime the rain was just one more strange thing that had happened before breakfast.
