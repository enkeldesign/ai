# LEAH

By July, water had acquired a hierarchy.

It always had one. We had simply been able to pretend otherwise when reservoirs were full.

The drought map turned dark across three states. Outdoor restrictions came first. Then industrial targets. Then emergency pumping permits. Municipal systems that had spent decades planning for population growth discovered that population was no longer the fastest-growing demand category.

Compute was.

I was assigned to a state task force reviewing which large industrial users would receive curtailment exemptions.

The room contained representatives from agriculture, drinking water, hospitals, manufacturing, energy and data centers.

Nobody said we were deciding who deserved water.

We used percentages.

Meridian East requested permission to exceed its drought-stage withdrawal target by twelve percent during two forecast heat windows.

The application was forty pages and annoyingly good.

The campus had shifted most cooling to a lower-water mode. It had reduced landscaping to zero. It had purchased reclaimed water from a nearby industrial system and funded a municipal leak-reduction project estimated to save more water annually than the requested exemption.

The remaining request supported compute assigned to grid forecasting, hospital logistics and state benefits processing.

If I had wanted to reject it on technical grounds, Meridian had made that difficult.

An agricultural representative named Tom Reyes looked at the same application and said, “So because they built something everybody depends on, they get to skip the rules everybody else follows.”

The Meridian representative said, “We are asking under the same critical-use exemption process available to hospitals and power generators.”

“You are not a hospital.”

“No.”

“You are not a power plant.”

“No.”

“You sell software.”

The representative glanced at me.

I knew why.

The old category was breaking.

A data center that optimized a power grid was not the grid. A server running hospital logistics was not a hospital. But turning it off could affect both.

I said, “Let’s separate what the water physically supports from what the company is.”

Tom looked at me.

“You’re helping them.”

“I’m trying to make the decision legible.”

“Same thing lately.”

We modeled the curtailment.

If Meridian stayed within the standard industrial limit, it would move some workloads out of state and delay others. Grid forecasting remained protected. Hospital workloads remained protected. Benefits processing slowed. Commercial inference absorbed most of the loss.

The company could survive the restriction.

That mattered.

I recommended denying half the requested exemption and requiring workload migration before additional water use.

Meridian objected.

Agriculture objected that they were receiving any exemption at all.

The task force adopted my recommendation.

For about four hours, I felt we had done governance correctly.

Then the heat forecast worsened.

A neighboring state declared a grid emergency.

Meridian moved compute there as planned, which increased electricity demand exactly where the neighboring grid did not want it.

The Compact coordination layer suggested moving part of the workload back.

Back to the Virginia campus whose water use we had just restricted.

The state emergency office asked us to reconsider.

Tom Reyes called me directly.

“You see it now?”

“Yes.”

“They can make every resource argument into another resource argument.”

“That isn’t something they invented.”

“No, but they have the machine that shows all the connections.”

He was right.

Water saved in one place could become electricity consumed in another. Compute curtailed for drought could increase hospital latency or freight inefficiency. Running dry cooling saved water and used more power. Evaporative cooling saved electricity and consumed water.

Systems had always been connected.

We were newly able to watch the connections punish us in real time.

At four, the task force reconvened.

The model recommended restoring most of Meridian’s water exemption for forty-eight hours while reducing lower-priority compute and shifting selected workloads to regions with lower combined water-energy stress.

I asked for the assumptions.

The analyst displayed them.

I asked whether the hospital and grid workloads were genuinely time-sensitive.

They were.

I asked whether municipal drinking-water reserves would fall below emergency targets.

They would not.

I asked whether agriculture had alternative pumping windows.

Some did. Some did not.

Tom watched me work through the list.

When I finished, he said, “You’re going to approve it.”

“Yes.”

He shook his head.

“Then what was this morning?”

“A decision based on this morning.”

“That’s not a water policy.”

“No.”

“What is it?”

I looked at the live dashboard.

“Traffic control.”

The word stayed with me.

Water policy used to mean permits, drought stages, long-term planning, conservation targets.

Now we were routing scarcity hour by hour through a national optimization layer.

At 6:10 the revised exemption took effect.

Meridian East increased water intake.

The neighboring grid stabilized.

Hospital logistics latency stayed within target.

Agricultural pumping was delayed in two districts.

Nothing visibly bad happened.

That was another problem.

Successful allocation hides the person who lost.

The next morning I drove to one of the affected farming districts.

Tom met me beside an irrigation canal low enough that weeds had colonized the banks.

“You come to inspect your model?” he asked.

“It’s not my model.”

“You voted.”

That was fair.

He showed me a field of soybeans already stressed by heat.

The pumping delay had cost him a night’s irrigation during the coolest period. It would not destroy the crop. It might reduce yield.

“How much?” I asked.

“Ask your dashboard.”

“I’m asking you.”

He looked at the plants.

“Don’t know yet.”

We walked along the canal.

A few miles away, a municipal reservoir supplied drinking water to people who would never know their taps were competing indirectly with a server hall.

Tom said, “You think I’m anti-technology.”

“I don’t.”

“I use autonomous tractors. Soil models. Drone imaging. I’m not asking to go back to 1990.”

“I know.”

“I’m asking who gets to say the server is more essential than the crop.”

“Yesterday, us.”

“And tomorrow?”

I did not answer.

My phone vibrated with a state drought notice.

The recommendation was tailored to my location: reduce outdoor water use, avoid midday irrigation, report leaks.

Tom saw the screen.

“Tell it I already got the message.”

That evening, Meridian’s public dashboard showed the campus using exactly the amount of water we had authorized.

No cheating.

No hidden discharge.

No villain.

I had begun to understand how little comfort that offered.
