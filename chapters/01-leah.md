# LEAH

The first thing I noticed was the water temperature.

I had been sent to Meridian East because somebody in Richmond had finally decided that a retention pond reading thirty-four degrees Celsius at four in the morning deserved a second opinion. By the time I arrived, the number had already been downgraded in three different email threads from *thermal excursion* to *instrument anomaly* to *unverified field condition*.

People who work around permits learn to pay attention to adjectives. Adjectives are where responsibility goes to hide.

The campus sat beyond a line of loblolly pine and raw red earth, six long buildings with no windows and more electrical infrastructure than some towns. The newest hall was still under construction. Floodlights washed the clay embankments white. Behind the security fence, cooling equipment rose in ranks from the roofs, all fans and housings and maintenance ladders.

At 6:17 my phone vibrated in the cup holder.

**Environmental safety advisory**

**Possible contaminated precipitation in your area within several minutes. Avoid direct contact. Keep vehicle windows closed. Do not use untreated surface water.**

The advisory carried the familiar state safety icon. That mattered. It meant I didn’t have to spend time wondering whether somebody at Meridian had found my number.

I tapped the details while the gate camera scanned my plate.

The notice had been assembled by the phone’s safety service from weather, air-quality and local environmental feeds. No agency had issued it. The system did that occasionally now—heat warnings before the county posted them, smoke alerts, flash-flood routing. Usually it was useful enough that nobody thought much about what had produced the recommendation.

What caught my eye was the phrase *possible particulate contamination*.

The sky over the east fence looked dull but harmless. Low cloud. No lightning. The public radar thumbnail showed a weak patch of green thirty miles west and nothing over me worth naming.

The guard leaned out of the booth.

“Morning.”

“Morning. Okafor, Water Resources.”

He checked the screen beside him. “You’re early.”

“So is your pond.”

That got the faint smile you give an inspector when you know the inspector thinks she’s funny.

The first drop landed on the windshield while he was printing my visitor badge.

I watched it spread.

It was dark enough that for a second I thought a bird had found me with impossible timing. Then another drop struck beside it, and a third. The wipers were off. Each one flattened into a translucent rust color against the glass.

The guard looked past me at the road.

“What the hell?”

Rain began with the soft irregular tapping you hear before a shower decides whether it means it. On the black hood of the county car, the drops looked almost brown. On the concrete apron beside the gate, where they gathered in shallow pits, the color brightened.

Red clay was my first thought.

My second was iron.

My third was that I had spent too many years around industrial sites to stand outside and speculate when I could be inside and sample.

“Stay under the booth,” I told the guard.

He had already put out a hand.

“Don’t.”

He stopped with his palm halfway beyond the roofline.

“We don’t know what’s in it,” I said. “That’s enough reason.”

I pulled forward ten feet and parked under the inspection canopy. The rain strengthened. It was not movie blood. It didn’t run down the windshield in theatrical red sheets. Most of it looked like dirty rain until it pooled along the wiper blades, where the water became a rusty crimson.

That was worse, in a way. Something physically possible is harder to dismiss.

I opened the field case from the back seat and put on nitrile gloves. The county had supplied me with a face shield I had never once used for rain. I left it in the box and took the sample bottles instead.

The guard had found his phone.

“Mine says contaminated precipitation too.”

“Same state advisory?”

“Looks like it.”

“Screenshot it.”

“For what?”

“For when somebody tells us later it never said that.”

He stared at me for half a second, then did it.

I set a sterile polypropylene bottle just beyond the canopy and let the rain hit the inside. The smell was faint—wet mineral, hot pavement, something metallic that might have been suggestion. The air itself carried another odor from the campus, a dry electrical heat mixed with diesel exhaust from somewhere behind the north halls.

My phone buzzed again.

The advisory had updated.

**Likely suspended mineral particulate. Avoid unnecessary exposure. Public agency assessment pending.**

There was a link marked **Why am I seeing this?**

I tapped it.

The safety service listed the signal categories it had used: regional particulate monitors, satellite aerosol products, local precipitation sensors, wind field estimates.

And one source identified only as:

**Meridian East roof aerosol feed — corroborating**

I read it twice.

The guard said, “Bad?”

“Maybe boring.”

“Doesn’t look boring.”

“I mean the explanation.”

I took a second sample.

Meridian had sensors all over the site. Every large industrial campus did. Environmental monitors were cheap compared with the equipment they protected. There was nothing supernatural about a private sensor seeing something before the county did.

What I did not understand was why the state safety service had access to it.

Three weeks earlier, when the retention pond temperature spike first appeared, Meridian’s environmental counsel had spent forty minutes on a video call explaining that the company’s facility telemetry was proprietary, segmented and unavailable to external systems without a formal data request.

I remembered the exact phrase because I had written it in my notes.

*Unavailable by design.*

The south service road curved behind the new construction hall. I drove slowly, wipers pushing reddish water to either side. Work had stopped. A dozen people stood beneath an awning near the temporary office, filming the rain. Somebody had already posted it, because my phone began surfacing local clips under the safety notice.

At the stormwater forebay I parked beside a county pickup that should not have been there.

Nobody was in it.

The rain had turned the bare soil on the embankment a darker version of itself. Virginia clay is already red because iron has been weathering there for longer than any company has existed. If wind had lifted enough fine material from the construction zone and mixed it with combustion aerosol, a shower could pull it back down. Add industrial dust. Add smoke from somewhere upwind. You could get this.

Rare did not mean impossible.

The outfall pipe emptied into a concrete channel at the head of the pond. Steam did not rise from it. That was one of the things I was afraid I would see, which meant I was relieved by its absence.

I lowered a calibrated probe into the flow.

33.1 C.

Ambient air was twelve.

I took the reading again.

33.0.

The pond itself, twenty yards downstream, was cooler: 28.7 near the surface where I measured it. Still wrong for dawn in March, but not the impossible bathtub number from the original logger.

That told me something useful. The heat was local. Somebody had put warm water into the storm system recently enough that the plume still had shape.

I crouched to photograph the outfall and saw three small fish caught in weeds near the concrete edge. Two were on their sides. One moved its gills when the water pushed it.

Temperature alone did not tell me what killed fish. Warm water held less oxygen; stagnant water, nutrient load, chemicals and a dozen other things could do the rest. I wrote *DO sample* in my field notes rather than inventing a cause.

The red rain stippled the page protector.

My phone rang.

The caller ID said **Mara Chen — Wire Service**.

I had never spoken to Mara Chen, but I knew the name. Most people who worked around data centers did. She had spent the previous year making herself unpopular with utilities by asking who exactly was paying for the new substations.

I let it ring twice before answering.

“This is Leah Okafor.”

“Ms. Okafor, Mara Chen. I’m sorry to call your personal number. I got it from the public duty roster.”

“That’s not my personal number.”

A pause.

“Good,” she said. “Then I’m less sorry.”

The rain clicked against my hood.

“What do you need?”

“I have video from three people outside Meridian East showing red precipitation. One says a state safety warning arrived before the rain began. I’m trying to establish whether there’s a public-health issue.”

“Then call public health.”

“I did.”

“And?”

“They’re trying to establish whether there’s a public-health issue.”

I looked at the outfall probe.

33.0.

Mara said, “Can you confirm you’re at the site?”

“I can confirm I’m not going to be your anonymous inspector standing dramatically in blood rain.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No comment on location.”

“Can you tell me whether red rain is physically plausible without anything exotic?”

That was a better question.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“Suspended mineral dust. Iron-rich particles. Smoke and industrial aerosol can contribute. Rain scavenges material out of the air.”

“So this could be normal?”

“I didn’t say normal.”

“Natural?”

“I didn’t say that either.”

Another pause, shorter this time.

“What did you say?”

“I said physically plausible.”

The fish in the weeds flexed once and went still.

Mara asked, “Do you have any reason to connect the precipitation to Meridian?”

I looked at the safety advisory again. The private feed sat there in plain text.

Meridian East roof aerosol feed — corroborating.

I could have told her. It was not classified. It was not even secret, exactly. It was simply a fact whose meaning I did not yet understand.

“No,” I said. “Not yet.”

That answer bothered me as soon as it left my mouth.

Not because it was false.

Because I had already started deciding which facts counted as explanation and which ones counted as trouble.

Mara thanked me and asked for permission to call again. I told her she didn’t need permission to call. I did not tell her I would answer.

At 7:03 the rain weakened.

By 7:11 it had become ordinary enough that you could imagine you had exaggerated the color.

At 7:18 the county issued its first official notice.

**Unusual precipitation containing elevated particulate matter has been reported in portions of the region. Avoid contact with visibly discolored runoff pending analysis.**

No mention of blood. No mention of Meridian. No mention of the warning that had reached our phones almost an hour earlier.

I stood under the tailgate of the county car and sealed the last sample.

On the other side of the pond, the Meridian buildings made their continuous low mechanical sound. Fans, pumps, power conversion, thousands of machines doing work I could not hear individually.

My phone still showed the explanation for the advisory.

I took a screenshot of that too.

Then I put the phone in airplane mode before driving the samples to the lab.

I could not have said why.
