# ZEKE

I did not call Priya Raman a martyr.

That disappointed almost everyone.

The livestream title was **WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT PRIYA RAMAN — AND WHAT WE DO NOT**.

Not my best work from an engagement perspective.

Two hundred thousand people joined anyway.

I sat at the desk in my studio with a Bible closed beside me.

I left it closed on purpose.

“Priya Raman worked in security and governance at Meridian,” I said. “She was involved in an internal review. She died in a vehicle crash hours after contacting people outside the company. Those are facts reported by multiple sources.”

Comments moved too fast to read individually.

I continued.

“There is currently no public evidence that Meridian, Julian Rook, an AI system, the federal government, Satan, the CIA, China, Russia, globalists, climate activists or the state of Virginia killed her.”

The comment stream slowed slightly at Satan.

“If evidence changes, we change what we say. Until then, grief is not permission to lie.”

I had spent fifteen years learning that certainty attracts people and qualifications lose them.

I was trying to unlearn it while my audience grew.

The contradiction was not subtle.

I told them what Jeremy had asked me to tell them: do not harass Priya’s family, do not contact the crash investigators, do not invent vehicle logs, do not turn a woman you did not know into a prop.

Then I opened the Bible.

The comments accelerated.

“This is the part some of you are waiting for,” I said. “You want me to tell you this proves the fifth seal.”

I read the passage about the souls under the altar crying out for justice.

Then I closed the book again.

“Priya Raman is not ours to assign a costume in a prophecy chart.”

That sentence cost me subscribers in real time.

Good.

Maybe.

I kept talking.

“The Bible does not teach that every dead dissident is one of these souls, or that every suspicious crash proves persecution. What the passage does tell us is that the demand for justice belongs inside the vision. The dead are not statistics. Their blood is not absorbed into history just because the powerful call the outcome necessary.”

I had written that line earlier.

I was still a professional communicator, however much I wanted to pretend otherwise.

A comment flashed large enough to catch my eye.

**ROOK IS THE WHITE HORSE AND BEAST. PICK A SIDE.**

I read it aloud.

“No.”

More comments.

“I am serious. Stop trying to make me say what the text does not say. The white rider is interpreted different ways. The Beast comes later in the vision. Maybe patterns overlap. Maybe they don’t. If your theology requires events to become simpler as they get closer to God, I have bad news about the Bible.”

That line gained subscribers back.

I hated knowing.

After the stream, my producer Lacey came into the room.

“You’re changing the channel.”

“I’m sitting in the same chair.”

“You know what I mean.”

She held up the analytics tablet.

“Viewers spike when you say Beast, Mark, Rook, blood, judgment. They leave when you talk about interpretive humility.”

“Then we need more interesting humility.”

“That sounds like a joke.”

“It’s a business problem.”

Lacey had been with me since the channel was a borrowed camera in a church office. She did not share all my theology and understood my incentives better than I did.

“You want to keep doing this?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I looked at the closed Bible.

The correct answer was vocation.

The honest answer included attention.

“Because people are scared,” I said.

“That’s not why you started.”

“No.”

“Why did you start?”

“Because I thought everybody else was reading Revelation wrong.”

“Still think that?”

“Yes.”

She smiled.

“At least one thing’s stable.”

My son called that evening.

Daniel never watched the livestreams live. He said it made him nervous to know I could say something stupid in front of half a million people before he had time to stop me.

“You did okay,” he said.

“High praise.”

“You didn’t say she was murdered.”

“Because I don’t know that she was.”

“Half your audience does.”

“I noticed.”

“Dad, do you understand what scares me?”

I knew better than to answer quickly.

“That I make frightened people more certain?”

“That you make uncertainty feel like a temporary inconvenience before your theory wins.”

I sat back.

He had inherited his mother’s ability to hurt me efficiently.

“Do I?”

“Yes.”

“I told them I don’t know.”

“But you always know where you think not knowing is going.”

That stayed with me.

We talked about school instead.

After the call I drove to the memorial gathering outside Meridian’s northern offices.

Priya’s family had asked that it remain nonpolitical.

It did not.

Nothing remained nonpolitical anymore.

Engineers stood beside anti-AI activists. Meridian employees held candles. A group from Jeremy’s organization carried no signs because he had told them not to. Christians prayed quietly near the back. Reporters circulated at the edges.

Julian Rook arrived without an announcement.

The crowd noticed anyway.

People parted.

He wore a black suit and no tie. He looked exhausted.

For a moment I understood the power of the man without any system analysis.

Attention bends around some people.

Julian went directly to Priya’s parents.

I could not hear what he said.

Her father struck him in the chest.

Not hard. Open hand.

The movement went through the crowd like an electrical pulse.

Security stepped forward.

Julian raised one hand and they stopped.

Priya’s father hit him again.

This time Julian caught his wrist.

Not aggressively. He held it between both hands and said something.

Her father collapsed against him.

Julian held him while he cried.

Cameras recorded everything.

I knew immediately how the images would be used.

Saint.

Manipulator.

Murderer embracing victim’s father.

Compassion under attack.

There was no gesture a public man could make that remained one thing.

I looked for Mara and saw her twenty feet away, not filming. Her phone was in her pocket.

That made me trust her more.

Jeremy stood near a tree at the edge of the gathering.

I joined him.

“You came,” I said.

“So did you.”

“You watch the stream?”

“Some.”

“Review?”

“You resisted your worst instincts.”

“Five stars.”

He kept watching Julian.

“Priya hated what people like you do with deaths.”

“I know.”

“She would hate this.”

“The memorial?”

“The meaning.”

I thought about that.

“She doesn’t get to control what her death means.”

Jeremy turned sharply.

“Careful.”

“I don’t mean we get to invent it. I mean death enters other people’s lives. They ask what it says about the world.”

“And you have a ready answer.”

“No. That’s the problem.”

He studied me.

Then he looked back at Julian.

“Do you think he killed her?” he asked.

“No.”

“You answer fast.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

“Do you think the system did?”

His face changed.

“I don’t know what that sentence means yet.”

“Neither do I.”

For once, neither of us enjoyed agreeing.

A woman began singing near the front.

Not a hymn I knew. Someone else joined. Then another.

The crowd quieted around the song.

Priya’s photograph stood on an easel beneath a row of flowers. In it she was laughing at something outside the frame.

I thought about the souls under the altar asking how long.

Not because I believed Priya was literally one of them.

Because grief makes that question natural.

How long before truth?

How long before judgment?

How long before someone is responsible?

Apocalyptic language becomes dangerous when it answers those questions too quickly.

It becomes necessary when ordinary language refuses to ask them at all.

Julian left before the song ended.

People watched him go.

I watched the people.
