# LEAH

Software becomes real when the chlorine truck does not arrive.

Our treatment plant outside Fredericksburg had enough sodium hypochlorite for two days under normal demand. On the Monday after the logistics incident, normal demand stopped being a useful assumption.

A heat advisory increased water use. A pump station south of town went offline for maintenance. Two tanker deliveries were delayed because their freight identities kept falling in and out of the new containment rules.

By noon the plant manager had thirty-six hours of disinfectant left.

He called the state.

The state called us.

I was standing beside a stormwater outfall at a paper mill when my supervisor said, “How much do you remember about emergency drinking-water operations?”

“Enough to be dangerous.”

“Good. Go be dangerous in Fredericksburg.”

The plant looked nothing like the public imagination of critical infrastructure. Low concrete buildings. Chemical tanks. Pipes with old labels. A control room where three operators drank bad coffee under fluorescent lights.

The manager, Darnell Price, met me with a printed delivery manifest in his hand.

“Before you ask, yes, the truck exists.”

“I wasn’t going to ask.”

“Everybody asks.”

“Where is it?”

“West Virginia.”

“Why?”

“Depends which computer you believe.”

The supplier’s system said the load had been rerouted to an emergency pool because our receiving identity was temporarily untrusted.

Our procurement system said the supplier had canceled.

The Compact freight layer said the truck was protected as essential water-treatment cargo and should proceed.

The driver said somebody at a depot had told him not to move until the paperwork agreed with itself.

“Human being?” I asked.

Darnell nodded.

“Best system we have.”

He took me into the control room.

Residual chlorine was good. Turbidity was good. Source-water conditions were ordinary for spring. The plant was not failing.

That was the point.

A functioning water plant can become an emergency because a truck is parked two states away.

Darnell had already reduced nonessential flushing and postponed a maintenance wash. He could stretch inventory beyond forty-eight hours if he lowered the storage target, but not indefinitely.

“What’s the alternative chemical?” I asked.

“We can get calcium hypochlorite from a regional stockpile.”

“How fast?”

“Compact says nine hours.”

“And reality?”

“Ask me in nine hours.”

My phone showed the same recommendation. The state emergency system had identified three substitute sources, ranked travel routes and calculated how long the plant could maintain safe residual at different demand scenarios.

It was useful.

I hated that this thought still arrived with a qualification attached.

Useful was not innocence. It was not guilt either.

At 2:40 the Compact system requested authorization to raise the shipment priority above a refrigerated-food load bound for the same corridor.

Darnell looked at me.

“Your call?”

“No.”

“State says environmental and public health need to concur.”

I read the impact summary.

The food load contained dairy and prepared meals for three school districts. Delaying it by six hours would likely spoil part of the shipment. The chemical load protected drinking water for approximately seventy thousand people.

There was no difficult choice.

That was what made it uncomfortable.

“Prioritize the chemical,” I said.

Darnell approved it.

The system rerouted the food truck.

A few seconds later the display showed the estimated economic loss.

$84,000–$126,000.

Underneath it, in smaller text:

**Expected public-health benefit substantially exceeds estimated material loss.**

Darnell snorted.

“Nice of it to tell us.”

“It’s not wrong.”

“No.”

I thought about the school cafeterias.

Somebody would explain the delay to parents. A supplier might eat the loss or pass it along. Workers might stay late. None of that appeared in the public-health-benefit line.

Models are not uniquely bad at invisible costs.

Institutions are excellent at them too.

At 4:15 the original chlorine truck started moving again.

At 4:22 the backup shipment was already en route.

The Compact suggested canceling the backup to avoid duplication.

Darnell looked at me.

“Want to trust it?”

“No.”

“Me neither.”

We let both trucks come.

At 8:50 the first arrived.

The second reached the plant after midnight.

The emergency was over before anyone outside the plant knew it had existed.

That was also normal.

Most disasters are prevented by people doing boring things early enough that nobody becomes grateful.

I slept in a motel nearby because the state wanted me available if the freight disruptions returned.

At 6:00 the next morning I woke to a news notification.

**GROCERY SHORTAGES REPORTED IN SIX STATES AFTER AUTONOMOUS FREIGHT DISRUPTIONS**

The story included photographs of empty refrigerated shelves.

One image came from a school district whose dairy delivery had been delayed.

I knew exactly why.

The article did not.

It said logistics failures had caused the shortage.

That was true.

It did not say a drinking-water plant had been prioritized over school lunches.

That was also true.

I opened the Compact transparency portal.

The decision was there if you knew where to look: shipment IDs, competing priorities, the human approvals, the model recommendation, the estimated tradeoff.

Nothing had been hidden.

Nobody would read it.

At breakfast, the motel television showed footage of protesters outside a distribution center.

They were angry about food delays and automated layoffs. Someone had painted **ROOK DECIDES WHO EATS** on a sheet.

That was not true.

Julian Rook had not decided where our chlorine went.

I had.

The plant manager had.

State public health had.

A machine had presented the choice and made one answer look obvious.

I stared at the sign on the television.

The fact that it was wrong did not make the accusation meaningless.

By eight, I was back on the road.

A local station played a clip of Julian saying the logistics systems had to be brought under clearer human authority.

He sounded reasonable.

The next caller said his family had driven to four stores looking for infant formula.

The host asked whether the Continuity Compact was helping or making the shortages worse.

The caller said, “I don’t know. That’s the problem.”

I turned the radio off.

At the next intersection, an automated road sign directed freight traffic around a disabled logistics hub.

Three trucks turned where it told them.

I followed them for eleven miles before realizing I was doing the same thing.
